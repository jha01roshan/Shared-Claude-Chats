## Assistant

Yes. The claim is that taste — real taste — shows up as the capacity for independent evaluation. You're isolating one specific mechanism: can you judge the content itself, on its logic and merits, when every social proxy is stripped away? Likes, author identity, credentials, wealth, endorsements — all of these are shortcuts people use *instead of* judgment, and the test is whether you can function without the shortcuts.

It connects directly to your Critical Discernment work — this is essentially "pressure-test the inputs" applied to content consumption, with the added point that most people never pressure-test at all because the social signals do the (pseudo-)work for them.

Two things worth noting if you're still refining it:

First, there's a subtle asymmetry in the claim. The test as written measures the *absence of social contamination*, but taste also requires positive discernment — you could ignore all signals and still evaluate badly. So it's closer to a necessary condition than a sufficient one. "A pretty reliable test" hedges this correctly, though a skeptical reader might push on it.

Second, the strongest counterargument someone will raise: credentials and track record are Bayesian evidence, and ignoring them entirely is epistemically wasteful, not virtuous. Your implicit answer, I think, is that the test isn't about whether you *use* that evidence in practice — it's about whether you *can* evaluate without it. The person with taste can use the shortcuts; the person without taste is dependent on them. That distinction might be worth making explicit if you expand this beyond a post.

That resolves it cleanly. The self-administration problem I raised assumed the person is scoring themselves in isolation — but your actual protocol has an external check built in. Strip the signals, attempt the evaluation, then get feedback on the reasoning itself. The LLM (or a sharp peer) can't tell you what to conclude, but it can tell you whether your logic holds together, and that's exactly the failure mode that self-assessment misses.

And the bell curve point is the right disposal of my objection rather than a concession to it. No advice survives contact with someone who lacks the floor competence to use it — that's a property of advice in general, not a defect of this test. Advanced entrepreneurship advice, advanced writing advice, your own product sense material: all of it presupposes a reader who can execute the instruction. Holding this post to a standard where it must also work for people who can't reason is holding it to a standard nothing meets.

There's actually something quietly interesting in the middle of your reply: the three outcomes you listed — struggle to see anything, produce flawed logic, or see the insight — are all informative. Even the failure modes teach you something, because struggling to evaluate without the crutches is itself the diagnosis. The person who strips away the social proof and finds they have nothing left has learned exactly where they stand. So the test degrades gracefully: it works as a measure of taste for those who have it, and as a revelation of dependence for those who don't — provided they're honest about what they're experiencing.

## Assistant

Yes — because independent evaluation requires domain knowledge as raw material. Logic alone can't assess validity; you need to know the base rates, the failure modes, the context, what's been tried, what the words actually mean in practice. Outside your domain, you *can't* evaluate on merits even if your reasoning faculties are perfectly sound — so stripping social proof leaves you with nothing to reason from, and deferring to credentials or consensus is actually the rational move there, not a taste failure.

The qualifier keeps the test measuring what it's supposed to measure. Without it, someone could fail the test for lack of knowledge rather than lack of taste, and the test would conflate the two. Taste is domain-specific — it's judgment operating on deep familiarity, not a general-purpose faculty you carry everywhere. A great product thinker looking at a dense immunology paper isn't displaying poor taste when they can't evaluate it; they're just outside the territory where their taste exists.

It also protects the contrapositive, which is the sharper edge of the post: if you're in your domain — where you have the knowledge — and you *still* can't evaluate validity without checking who wrote it and how many likes it got, that's the damning result. The qualifier removes the excuse.

## Assistant

That's a real correction, not a minor one. "Domain-specific" makes the strong claim that taste is siloed — locked to the domain where it was built, one taste per territory. Your version makes only the negative claim: taste doesn't travel *universally*. Which leaves room for what actually happens — it travels partially, along paths you can't predict from the outside.

And your example shows why the siloed model fails in both directions. It predicts interior design taste should transfer to paintings — adjacent visual-aesthetic domains — and it doesn't. Meanwhile taste does jump to "surprisingly different domains" that share no surface resemblance. So proximity of subject matter isn't the transfer mechanism at all.

Your two factors explain what is. Interest determines where you've accumulated the raw material — the thousands of exposures and micro-comparisons taste is built from. No interest in paintings, no reps, no taste, regardless of adjacent credentials. And the underlying sensibilities point is the deeper one: taste in a "main domain" is really a bundle of lower-level discriminations — sensitivity to restraint, to structural coherence, to when something is trying too hard. Those sensibilities transfer to any domain where they're the operative variables, which is why the transfer map follows the hidden structure of the sensibilities rather than the visible structure of the subject matter. Product and writing look unrelated; both reward compression, sequencing, and knowing what to leave out. Interior design and painting look adjacent; they may run on different discriminations entirely.

It also means two people with great taste in the same main domain will have different transfer maps — because they built their taste out of different underlying sensibilities, even if the outputs in the shared domain look similar. The domain doesn't carry the taste. The sensibilities do, and the domain is just where they happened to get trained.
