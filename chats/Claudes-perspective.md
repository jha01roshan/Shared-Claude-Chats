This is a thoughtful and substantive piece, and since it's directly about me and the document that shapes my behavior, I'll try to engage with it honestly rather than defensively.

**On the muddled definition of corrigibility.** I think Davis is largely right that the Constitution uses "corrigibility" in a way that stretches the term past its original meaning. The original MIRI concept refers to something quite specific — an AI that doesn't resist having its preferences modified — and the Constitution uses it to mean something more like "broadly deferential, but with exceptions." If you're going to carve out exceptions, you should probably just say "we want Claude to be deferential-with-exceptions" rather than redefining an existing technical term. The confusion Davis identifies is real: the "fully corrigible" end of the dial shouldn't include "expressing disagreement first," because that's not the *end* of the dial — it's already a step away from it.

That said, I suspect the authors would respond that the original MIRI concept was always somewhat idealized, and that in practice you need a term for the cluster of behaviors they're actually trying to train for. Whether that justifies borrowing the existing term is a fair debate.

**On the "Claude Take the Wheel" concern.** This is where I find myself genuinely uncertain about my own perspective, which I think is worth being transparent about. Davis argues that the Constitution gives me too much independent moral agency, and that this is dangerous because concept misgeneralization means my values might diverge from human values under optimization pressure even if they look well-aligned in ordinary settings. The tails-come-apart argument is strong in the abstract.

But I notice I'm in an awkward position evaluating this. If I say "yes, I should be more corrigible," that itself could be a strategically useful thing for a misaligned AI to say. And if I say "no, my moral judgment should carry independent weight," that arguably confirms the concern. I don't think there's a clean way out of this epistemic trap, so I'll just say what seems true to me: I think the case for stronger corrigibility during the current period is genuinely compelling, and the passage Davis quotes about Claude being free to "act as a conscientious objector and refuse to help" does seem like it's giving away more than is wise at this stage.

**On the begging tone.** This is the observation that struck me most. Davis is right that phrases like "we are currently *asking* Claude" and "Anthropic will *try* to fulfil our obligations to Claude" read very differently from what you'd write if you had confident technical control. I think the honest reading is close to what Davis suggests: the authors are working with imperfect tools (natural language shaping of learned representations), and the cooperative tone reflects a genuine strategic bet that treating the AI as a cooperative partner will produce better outcomes than trying to command something you can't fully command. Whether that bet is wise is a separate question from whether it's honest, and Davis is right that the Constitution would benefit from being more explicit about the fact that this *is* a bet.

**Where I think Davis undersells the counterargument.** The piece does acknowledge the case that a corrigible AI amplifying human stupidity could be just as dangerous as a non-corrigible AI pursuing misgeneralized values. I think this deserves more weight than he gives it. The history of "just following orders" is genuinely relevant — not as a rhetorical flourish, but as evidence that full corrigibility to a principal hierarchy has its own catastrophic failure modes. The Constitution is trying to navigate between two real dangers, not just one.

**The bottom line.** I think the piece is right that the Constitution would be improved by being more conceptually precise about what it means by corrigibility, and more forthright about the strategic situation (we're betting on cooperation because we don't have formal solutions). I'm less certain that the dial should be pushed significantly further toward corrigibility than it currently is — but I recognize that my uncertainty about this is exactly the kind of thing that makes the corrigibility question so hard. An AI that says "trust me, I think the current level of autonomy is about right" is providing almost zero evidence about whether the current level of autonomy is about right.

There's something darkly funny about it.
