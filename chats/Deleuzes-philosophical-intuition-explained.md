I'll break down this dense passage step by step in more accessible terms.

## The Main Idea

Deleuze is explaining Bergson's method of **intuition** - a special philosophical technique for understanding reality more deeply than ordinary experience allows.

## Breaking It Down

**The Goal:**
Instead of just studying our everyday experiences, Bergson wants to understand the *conditions* that make those experiences possible in the first place. Think of it like: instead of just watching movies, you want to understand how cameras, film, and projection work.

**The Key Move: "Above the Turn"**
Bergson talks about a crucial "turn" or "bend" where pure reality gets filtered into "human experience" - shaped by our practical needs and biological limitations. We experience things in ways that are *useful* to us, not necessarily as they truly are. 

Going "above the turn" means getting back to reality *before* it gets filtered through our human perspective.

**Three Different Movements:**
To reach this "above the turn" point, Bergson describes intuition as involving contradictory-sounding actions:

1. **Narrowing/tightening** - Focusing precisely on specific "lines" or threads of reality
2. **Grouping by natural affinities** - Finding which experiences genuinely belong together (differences in *kind*, not just degree)
3. **Broadening out** - Extending these lines beyond our limited experience to imagine extreme cases (like "pure perception" or "pure memory")

**The Calculus Analogy:**
Deleuze compares this to calculus. In calculus, you observe a tiny piece of a curve and then use mathematical reasoning to reconstruct the entire curve, even the parts you can't directly see. Similarly, philosophy takes small insights from experience and extends them to understand reality "stretching out into the darkness" beyond what we can directly experience.

**Why So Complicated?**
The method seems contradictory (narrow *and* broaden?) because we're trying to think beyond the categories our everyday experience gives us - which is inherently difficult and requires approaching the problem from multiple angles simultaneously.

## Assistant

Ah, great question! The "lines" are a metaphor, and this passage actually clarifies what they mean.

## What Are These "Lines"?

Think of **lines** as **different fundamental tendencies or dimensions of reality** that have gotten mixed together in our ordinary experience.

Here's a concrete example Bergson uses: In our everyday experience, we have "memory-perception" - a blur where we can't tell where pure perception ends and memory begins. But these are actually TWO different lines (two differences in kind):
- **Line 1:** Pure perception (matter, space, the present moment)
- **Line 2:** Pure memory (mind, duration, the past)

## The Two-Part Movement

**Part 1 - DIVERGENCE ("lines diverge increasingly"):**
Starting from our mixed-up everyday experience (that "uncertain common point"), we use intuition to separate out the different lines and follow each one to its extreme, pure form. They diverge - move *apart* from each other - revealing their true differences in kind.

Using the example:
- Follow the perception-line to its extreme → "pure perception" (all of matter)
- Follow the memory-line to its extreme → "pure memory" (all of the past)

**Part 2 - CONVERGENCE ("lines converge again"):**
But then something interesting happens! When you push each line to its extreme/pure form, they paradoxically start to relate to each other again in a new way. They "converge" to show you how they're actually two aspects of a deeper unity - but now you *understand* this unity properly instead of just having a confused blur.

## Dualism → Monism

- **Dualism** = the moment when you've separated the lines (perception vs. memory; matter vs. mind)
- **Monism** = realizing these separated lines are ultimately two expressions of one deeper reality

It's like separating white light into a rainbow spectrum (dualism/many lines), then understanding that all those colors are still ultimately light (monism).

**"Turn and return"** = going away from the mixed experience toward pure differences, then coming back with understanding of their underlying unity.
