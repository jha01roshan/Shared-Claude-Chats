No images came through on my end — I'll take "the abstract paintings" to mean the neoplastic corpus, roughly 1919 onward, and if you do have specific canvases in mind, send them and I'll get more concrete.

The useful thing about Mondrian is that the vocabulary is fixed and public: three primaries, three non-colors, orthogonals, no diagonal, no curve, no depth cue. Everything that usually carries a painting — drawing, subject, iconography, atmosphere — has been stripped out, so quality reduces almost entirely to judgments about interval, proportion, and edge. There is nowhere to hide, which is why the variance across his output is so wide and why connoisseurship here is unusually testable: two works can share the entire vocabulary and differ enormously.

The diagnostics I'd apply:

**The crop.** This is the single most reliable tell. An excellent Mondrian reads as a fragment arbitrarily cut from an infinite field — lines run off the edge, and the color planes are pushed to the periphery and truncated by the framing edge. A merely good one is self-enclosed: color sits comfortably inside the picture, surrounded by white, and the composition resolves into an emblem. A red square in the middle is a target. A red plane jammed into a corner and clipped generates torque.

**Instability.** The best pictures balance, but just barely, and asymmetrically. You feel that shifting any element two centimeters would wreck it. That's the actual test — perform the translation mentally. With a pretty good Mondrian, you can't tell the difference.

**Interval range.** Excellent works run very narrow slots against very large fields with no repeating module and no ratio you could name. Weaker ones drift toward approximate halves and thirds, near-repetition, a rhythm the eye resolves in half a second and then has no reason to return to. The 1917–19 grid works fail in this direction, and Mondrian knew it — the systematic lattice was exactly what he spent the 1920s escaping.

**Color as weight rather than accent.** In the strong pictures the areas are inversely tuned to optical weight: a small yellow holds against a large white and a mid-sized blue. In the weak ones the primaries appear in roughly comparable patches, evenly distributed, which is decoration rather than calibration.

**Active white.** In the best works the whites are figures, not ground — differentiated in temperature and scale, each one doing structural work. In lesser ones white is inert filler between black lines.

**Facture.** Underrated, and invisible in reproduction. Great Mondrians are physically dense: layered whites, visible brush, hand-drawn edges with slight wobble, black lines built up over multiple campaigns and sometimes scraped back. This is why the paintings that reproduce most cleanly are not reliably the best ones, and why an excellent Mondrian is startling in person — it looks handmade, almost laborious. Thin, mechanical surfaces usually mean a picture he solved rather than fought.

**Risk.** The 1929–33 works that leave enormous white expanses to be held by one small color patch, or that substitute a doubled line — a slot of white where a line should be — could have failed outright. A pretty good Mondrian takes no such risk; it restates a solution he'd already reached. The mid-to-late 1930s produce a fair number of these, dry and competent.

Two complications worth flagging. The lozenge paintings can flatter: rotating the support forces the orthogonal grid into conflict with the edges and manufactures crop drama automatically, so the format does work the composition hasn't earned — though a few of the diamonds are also among his best. And the New York pictures are a genuine second peak on different terms, since abandoning black line means the criteria above partly stop applying; there the question becomes whether the mosaic sustains a pulse or dissolves into fussiness, and both outcomes occur.
