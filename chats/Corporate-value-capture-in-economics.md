The canonical answer is **~2.2%**, from Nordhaus's 2004 paper *Schumpeterian Profits in the American Economy*. Looking at U.S. nonfarm business 1948–2001, he estimated that producers capture about 2.2% of the social surplus from innovation; the other ~97.8% flows to consumers as lower prices and better products.

That headline number decomposes into two pieces: an instantaneous appropriability of ~7% (the share innovators grab in year one) combined with ~20%/year depreciation of those rents as patents expire, imitators arrive, and successors leapfrog.

A few caveats worth keeping in mind before quoting it at a dinner party:

The 2.2% is *surplus from innovation*, not "all value corporations create." Rents from market power, brand, regulatory moats, and labor exploitation aren't in the numerator. So the figure underestimates total corporate capture if your question is broader than Schumpeterian profits.

It's an economy-wide average that masks huge variance. Pharma with strong patents looks very different from commodity software or weather forecasting (where appropriability rounds to zero). For any individual firm or sector, 2.2% is nearly meaningless.

It depends on TFP measurement, which is famously noisy, and on a model where the counterfactual is competitive pricing of the old technology — both contestable.

Replications and follow-ups (Hall et al. 2005; Kogan, Papanikolaou, Seru, Stoffman 2017 on patent values; Jones & Summers 2020 on the social returns to R&D) all land in the same neighborhood: innovators capture a single-digit-to-low-double-digit percentage of the value they create. Jones & Summers in particular calculate marginal social returns to R&D investment that imply we're badly underinvesting — consistent with low private capture.

Bottom line: 2.2% is the number to cite, with "from innovation, per Nordhaus" as the asterisk.
