Having read the paper: your framing is actually slightly more demanding than what Birks proposes, but the gap you're pointing at is real and I think it's the fatal one. Birks never requires the dust to accompany the meteor down — he just asserts that what is left of an ablated meteor that terminates in the lower troposphere is a trail or cloud of dust and then spends all his effort on keeping an *already-formed* compact cloud together (magnetic dendrites, few-kT dipole bonds). The step from "trail" to "1-m ball" is simply skipped, and that step looks dynamically prohibited, not merely rare.

The kinematics: an ablated particle stops relative to the air over a distance of roughly (ρ_particle/ρ_air)·d. For a 100 nm iron particle in the troposphere that's under a millimeter; even a 10 μm particle stops within ~10 cm. Meanwhile the parent body is moving at km/s during any phase in which it's hot enough to shed anything. So the dust cannot stay near the meteor even for a millisecond — it is deposited as a static column strung along the flight path. If the body sheds 500 g during a 0.5 s terminal flare at 3 km/s, that mass is distributed over ~1.5 km of path. To get it inside 1 m instead, you'd need the shedding to happen while the body moves at ~1 m per shedding-timescale — i.e., after it has essentially stopped. But a stopped body is in dark flight, cold at the surface (the reason freshly fallen meteorites are famously cool to the touch), no longer producing dust at all. The regime "slow enough to deposit compactly" and the regime "hot enough to ablate" are mutually exclusive under standard entry physics, and there's no continuous parameter — size, velocity, entry angle, composition — that interpolates between them. That's why I'd say the Goldilocks fraction here isn't 10⁻⁸, it's structurally zero absent new physics.

There's also an altitude mismatch stacked on top. Ablation and terminal flares end at ~15–30 km even for deep-penetrating iron bodies, while the orbs are reported near cloud level or lower. So even granting a compact dust blob at the terminal point, it must descend 15+ km through increasingly turbulent air, over hours at aerosol settling speeds, while held together by bonds of a few kT — in an environment where nocturnal-boundary-layer eddy diffusivities (~0.01–0.1 m²/s) would disperse an unconfined 1-m cloud in tens of seconds. Birks himself flags this: whether such weakly bonded aggregates can persist in the presence of atmospheric turbulence and convective motion remains uncertain.

And we have a direct observational check on what meteor dust actually does: persistent trains. They behave exactly as the kinematics predicts — tens-of-km filaments that expand and get wind-sheared into contorted ribbons within minutes. If some rare configuration produced compact self-binding dust balls, you'd expect trained meteor photographers to occasionally capture the intermediate cases: a train contracting, or a blob detaching. Nobody has. The alternative route — the trail *later* self-concentrates via magnetic flocculation — fails on volume: going from a kilometer-scale deposit to a 1-m ball is a ~10⁹ contraction in volume against turbulence, and flocculation of magnetized grains produces fast-settling clumps (Birks even uses this to explain how non-magnetic material is *lost*), not a levitating sphere.

So my answer to your question: the "cloud stays near the falling meteor" picture fails immediately on relative velocities, for all meteors, not just most. The paper's correlation statistics (his ~3σ orb–fireball coincidence) only require conversion efficiencies of ~10⁻⁸, so he can hide almost anything in the Goldilocks appeal — but rare-parameter arguments only work when the required configuration lies somewhere in the parameter space, and here it seems not to. The honest version of the hypothesis needs a concentration mechanism as its centerpiece, and the paper doesn't contain one. Notably, he half-concedes this by leaning on the Genesis-2 reentry video and the Council Bluffs iron mass (terrestrial isotopes, low nickel — i.e., a booster, not a meteorite): slow, massive, artificial reentries with minutes-long entry times are the one class of object that plausibly *can* shed material while moving slowly at low altitude. That's a more defensible paper, but it can't supply anywhere near 508 orbs from ~820 mostly-ocean reentries a year.

Is it possible that a falling meteor induces adjacent turbulent flows where large enough eddies could collect enough particles  to make such orbs?

---

## Assistant

Turbulent concentration of particles is a real phenomenon, so the question is fair — but the known mechanisms all point the wrong way for this application, and there's a clean two-horned argument for why.

The relevant physics is preferential concentration, governed by the Stokes number St = τ_p/τ_flow, the ratio of particle response time to eddy turnover time. Heavy particles with St ~ 1 do cluster: vortices centrifuge them outward and they pile up in the strain regions between eddies. But two caveats kill it here. First, the clusters are fractal sheets and filaments at the dissipation scale (mm–cm in atmospheric turbulence), with local enhancement factors of maybe 10–100, transient over an eddy turnover time — not a persistent meter-scale ball. What you'd need is a concentration factor of order 10⁴ (500 g strung along a kilometer of trail collected into 1 m³) held for an hour, which is a different beast entirely. Second, and this is the horn Birks can't escape: vortices *expel* heavy particles from their cores. Eddies collect bubbles and light particles; they fling iron grains out. There is no "eddy that gathers dense dust into its center."

Now the two-horned part. For the orb to float and glow for an hour, the grains must be nanometric — that's central to Birks's own model, since only nano-particles have negligible settling speeds and the surface-to-volume ratio for slow pyrophoric burn. But a 100-nm iron grain has τ_p ~ 10⁻⁶ s, so in boundary-layer turbulence (τ_η ~ 0.1 s) its Stokes number is ~10⁻⁵: it is a perfect tracer. And a tracer field in incompressible turbulence can only ever be advected, stretched, and mixed — scalar variance decays monotonically; turbulence dilutes tracers, it never un-mixes them. So the particles small enough to make a levitating hour-long orb are exactly the particles that no eddy, of any size, can concentrate at all. Conversely, grains big enough to be concentrated (St ~ 1 at those dissipation rates means tens of microns) settle at cm/s to m/s and are gone from the column in minutes. The floatability requirement and the concentratability requirement exclude each other — same structure as the shedding problem from before, where "hot enough to ablate" excluded "slow enough to deposit compactly."

The one flow structure that genuinely travels with the body is the attached recirculation bubble in its near wake — that's the closest thing to your intuition of "adjacent flow that holds particles near the meteor." But it's body-sized (a 1-m bubble needs a ~0.5-m meteorite, which then makes a crater someone finds), it exchanges fluid with the outer wake on timescales of D/U ~ milliseconds so entrained dust leaks continuously, it exists during dark flight precisely when the body is cold and shedding nothing, and it's destroyed at the moment of impact when the wake overtakes the stopped body and blasts past it — dispersing, not depositing, whatever it carried.

The best steelman in this family is not an eddy but a buoyant vortex ring: a terminal airburst can roll up into a coherent dust-laden ring or thermal, and those genuinely do transport particulates as a compact structure over long distances — Etna's volcanic vortex rings persist for minutes and travel kilometers. But meteor terminal bursts happen at 15–30 km, the resulting structure is a rising toroid or mushroom cap tens of meters across rather than a 1-m sphere at treetop height, and observed vortex-ring lifetimes are minutes against the required hour. So even the most generous flow-structure candidate misses on altitude, geometry, scale, and lifetime simultaneously. I'd conclude the turbulence route doesn't rescue the model — it runs into the same signature problem, that every requirement for the orb's persistence directly negates a requirement for its assembly.

## Assistant

Here are five, in increasing order of how much I like them. Numbers are order-of-magnitude; I flag the load-bearing coefficients.

**1. Dust freezes into the air within centimeters.** A shed grain decelerates relative to air over its "billiard" stopping length ℓ ≈ (ρ_p/ρ_air)·d — the distance to sweep up its own mass. For 100 nm iron (ρ_p ≈ 7900 kg/m³) at 20 km (ρ_air ≈ 0.09): ℓ ≈ 1 cm; at sea level, ~1 mm. Even 10 μm grains stop within meters. So the parent, moving at km/s, abandons its dust instantly; the deposit geometry is a static column whose length is set entirely by v·Δt_shed. This is the quantitative version of "no cloud accompanies the fall," and it's robust to every choice of grain size the model is allowed.

**2. Required linear deposition density = required dynamic pressure = explosion.** This one converts the Goldilocks exclusion into an identity. Ablative mass loss is set by convective heating: dm/dt = ½C_H ρ_air v³ A / Q_v, with C_H ≈ 0.1 and Q_v ≈ 7 MJ/kg to vaporize iron. The linear density laid down along the track is therefore λ = (dm/dt)/v = C_H·A/(2Q_v) · ρ_air v² — i.e., λ is proportional to the dynamic pressure. Demanding a compact deposit (say 100 g within ~1 m, λ ≈ 0.1 kg/m) from a 10-cm body (A ≈ 0.03 m²) forces ρ_air v² ≈ 4.7×10⁸, i.e., v ≈ 20 km/s *at sea level*, dynamic pressure ~230 MPa. That exceeds the crushing strength of even iron meteoroids (~10–100 MPa) by an order of magnitude, and the body's stopping length m/(½ρC_D A) ≈ 200 m means it dumps its full ~10⁹ J (a 4-kg body at 20 km/s ≈ 0.2 t TNT) within a couple hundred meters: a Chelyabinsk-style terminal airburst, not a quiescent 1-m ball. The only corner of (v, ρ_air) space that satisfies the deposition-density requirement is the corner that is observationally a bomb. Conversely, at any survivable λ: the most generous "still shedding" floor — radiative balance ½C_H ρv³ ≥ σT⁴ at mere melting (1811 K) — needs v ≳ 220 m/s at sea level, and at 220 m/s the heat-flux-limited λ is ~10⁻⁵ kg/m, four orders short. The exclusion is two-sided and has no seam.

**3. Nanoparticles cannot be turbulently concentrated, at any eddy scale.** Particle response time with slip correction: τ_p = C_c ρ_p d²/(18μ) ≈ 7×10⁻⁷ s for 100 nm iron. Kolmogorov time in a nocturnal boundary layer (ε ~ 10⁻³ m²/s³): τ_η = √(ν/ε) ≈ 0.1 s. So St ≈ 10⁻⁵ — and since preferential-concentration enhancement scales as St² in this regime, the density perturbation is ~10⁻¹⁰. The grains are exact tracers, and tracer variance under incompressible advection–diffusion decays monotonically; there is no un-mixing term to exploit. Running the requirement backward: St ~ 1 needs τ_p ~ 0.1 s, i.e., d ≈ 70 μm — whose settling speed v_s = τ_p·g ≈ 1 m/s empties a 100-m column in ~2 minutes and the orb's own half-meter in a fraction of a second. The floatable and the concentratable populations are separated by three decades in diameter.

**4. Energy budget of the magnetic network vs. turbulent power throughput.** Take Birks's numbers at face value: 100 g of 100-nm grains is N ≈ m/(ρ_p·πd³/6) ≈ 2×10¹⁶ particles, each with O(1) dipole bonds at ~5 kT ≈ 2×10⁻²⁰ J, so total network binding energy ~5×10⁻⁴ J. Turbulence delivers kinetic energy through the 1 m³ occupied volume at ρ_air·ε·V ≈ 10⁻³ W for boundary-layer ε ~ 10⁻³ m²/s³. So the ambient flow supplies the *entire* cohesive energy of the structure every ~0.5 s; surviving an hour requires ε ≲ 10⁻⁷ m²/s³, one to two orders below even the calmest stable nocturnal boundary layer. Equivalent diffusion phrasing: persistence for 3600 s at L = 1 m needs effective diffusivity below L²/t ≈ 3×10⁻⁴ m²/s, i.e., near-molecular — while a body that drifts, hovers, and "responds" to observers is by hypothesis embedded in moving air.

**5. Wake-bubble residence time.** The one flow structure that does co-move is the recirculating near wake, but its fluid exchanges with the free stream at entrainment velocity ~0.05U across the bubble. Residence time τ ≈ D/(0.05U): for a 10-cm body in dark flight at 100 m/s, τ ≈ 20 ms. A 30-s descent recycles the bubble ~10³ times; the retained mass fraction is e^(−1000). To make τ comparable to the descent you need D/U ~ 10 s, i.e., a kilometer-scale body — at which point the missing crater is the least of the problems.

The strongest of these is 2, because it's not a parameter estimate but a structural proportionality: compact deposition *is* high dynamic pressure, and high dynamic pressure *is* fragmentation. If you wanted to publish a critique, I'd lead with 2, use 3 as the independent kill of the turbulence rescue, and 4 as the kill of the persistence claim even granting assembly — three separately sufficient exclusions, each attacking a different stage (assembly, concentration, survival). Worth double-checking before leaning hard: the C_H ≈ 0.1 heat-transfer coefficient in calc 2 (it drops at low altitude/high Re, which only worsens the problem for Birks) and the St² clustering scaling in calc 3, which is well-established for St ≪ 1 but worth citing properly (Chun et al. 2005 or Reade & Collins).

Are there places where wind swept magnetized dust might collect together, and then be ignited by lightning?

## Assistant

There are such places, and this pathway is much stronger than the meteor one — it inverts the assembly problem correctly. Instead of demanding that a km/s source deposit compactly in real time, you let aeolian and hydraulic sorting do the concentration over months to millennia. Density sorting genuinely works: black-sand placers on beaches and dune crests take magnetite from ~1% of bulk sediment to near-pure layers holding kilograms per square meter — concentration factors of 10²–10³, incomparably beyond anything in the fireball scenario. Candidate sites: beach and river placers, desert lag surfaces and dry lakebeds, volcanic ash plains (Icelandic and Andean ash runs several percent titanomagnetite), laterites and banded-iron terrain, plus anthropogenic analogs like fly-ash heaps and steel-mill fallout. All at ground level, where the orbs are actually reported — the altitude mismatch that plagued the meteor route vanishes for free.

But note the same two-horned structure stalks this version too, just one horn at a time now. Wind sorting is preferential concentration writ large: saltation and settling discriminate by density only for grains with appreciable inertia and fall speed, i.e., ~50–500 μm sand. Nanoparticles are tracers in aeolian transport for the identical St ≪ 1 reason as in calc 3 — they follow streamlines, never sort, and deposit diffusely. So wind collects exactly the grains too heavy to levitate, and the floatable fraction never collects. Worse, the collectable material is chemically spent: magnetite is already three-quarters oxidized. The residual energy in 4Fe₃O₄ + O₂ → 6Fe₂O₃ is ~0.5 MJ/kg, versus 7.4 for metallic iron and ~32 for silicon. A lightning strike that merely *ignited* a pre-existing magnetite drift would get a brief surface fizzle.

The rescue is that lightning doesn't ignite the deposit — it remanufactures it, and this is a published mechanism with a spectroscopic confirmation. Abrahamson and Dinniss (Nature, 2000) proposed that a cloud-to-ground strike on soil carbothermally reduces silica and iron oxides (soil carbon as reductant, the same chemistry as an arc furnace), and the metal vapor recondenses into nanometer Si/Fe particles that aggregate into exactly the kind of filamentary aerogel network Birks wants — born compact at the strike point, glowing by slow reoxidation. In 2014, Cen, Yuan and Xue serendipitously caught ball lightning on a slit spectrograph in Qinghai immediately after a ground strike: the spectrum showed Si, Fe, and Ca lines — soil composition — which is about as direct a confirmation of the soil-vaporization pathway as one could ask. And the mass budget matches beautifully: fulgurite formation shows a single strike melts grams to kilograms of sediment and vaporizes plausibly 10–100 g, which is precisely Birks's required orb payload. Vapor condensation manufactures the nano-sizes that no transport process could collect; reduction recharges the chemistry (10 g of Si at 32 MJ/kg is 320 kJ — enough to radiate 100 W for close to an hour, so the energy budget for long persistence is actually there); a magnetite-enriched placer stacks the odds by giving the strike an iron-rich, low-melting target. So a strike on a black-sand beach or wet volcanic ash flat is, on paper, the best natural orb factory anyone has proposed.

The residual gap is lifetime, and it's kinetic rather than energetic: the Qinghai ball lasted 1.6 s, ball-lightning surveys cluster below ~10 s with a thin tail to minutes, and Abrahamson's own oxidation kinetics predict seconds to tens of seconds because the network burns and disperses far faster than the energy-limited hour. So this mechanism comfortably buys you seconds-scale glowing soil-plasma balls and strains to reach minutes — three orders shy of Birks's hour, though now the shortfall is one kinetic rate (oxygen-diffusion-limited burn plus mechanical shredding) rather than a chain of impossibilities.

What I'd flag as most useful to you: the two hypotheses now make cleanly discriminating predictions. Birks predicts orbs correlate with fireball trajectories and nothing else; the lightning-placer mechanism predicts orbs correlate with the product of ground-strike density and surface iron/silicon availability — beaches, volcanic terrain, iron ranges, storm seasons — and with fresh fulgurites recoverable at the site. Hessdalen, the one persistently orb-producing locality with instrumented monitoring, sits in a valley of pyrite and iron-zinc mineralization (Monari's group even proposed the valley works as a geochemical battery), which is at least suggestive that mineralized ground, not meteors, is the common thread. A geographic regression of orb reports against lightning climatology and soil geochemistry versus against bolide tracks would adjudicate — and is exactly the kind of test the Birks paper never offers.

## Assistant

This reply has the structure of answering the objection adjacent to the fatal one. Trail width and sticking efficiency were never the problem — near-unity sticking for magnetic nanoparticles is fine, and tens-of-meters trail width is plausible. The problem is the longitudinal mass budget, and coagulation is powerless against it because coagulation conserves mass locally: an aggregate can only gather what diffuses to it. Brownian displacement for 100-nm grains is √(2Dt) ~ millimeters per hour; differential settling among nanoparticles is nil. So each nascent orb collects from a neighborhood of at most ~meters, and orb mass is bounded by λ × (a meter or so), where λ is the linear deposition density from before. Even taking the most generous survivable case — a strong iron body flaring at 10–15 km, λ ~ grams per meter — each bead gets grams, not his 100–500 g. A gram of iron holds 7.4 kJ; radiated over an hour that's 2 W, a candle at close range, invisible at kilometers. Notably, the beads-on-a-string picture is itself a concession to the deposition argument: he's given up assembling one 100-g ball and distributed the mass along the trail — but the whole reason the single ball was impossible (mass strung out longitudinally) now reappears as each bead being three orders of magnitude too small. The mass didn't get collected; it got relabeled.

His coagulation timescale is right in a window that doesn't help him. τ_coag ≈ 2/(Kn) with K ~ 3×10⁻¹⁶ m³/s for 100-nm grains. In the fresh wake — the first fraction of a second, while the trail is centimeters wide — n can hit 10¹¹–10¹²/cm³ and coagulation runs in milliseconds. That's real, and it's the standard formation story of meteoric smoke: it produces micron-scale fractal aggregates strung along the trail, which is exactly what's observed in persistent trains. But once the trail expands to his "tens of meters," the same gram-per-meter mass gives n ~ 10⁶/cm³ and τ_coag ~ an hour — while turbulent dilution drops n like t⁻¹ and coagulation rate goes as n². Dilution wins; growth freezes out at microns. There is no regime in which Brownian coagulation, at any sticking efficiency, crosses six decades of scale from smoke aggregate to meter-ball. The fast window and the wide trail are different epochs, and he's borrowing the rate from one and the geometry from the other.

The blinking and splitting are epicycles, and expensive ones. A smoldering pyrophoric network is a thermal-inertia object; "blinking out" means quenching, and a quenched iron nanoparticle passivates — the oxide shell that stops the burn doesn't dissolve when conditions improve, so re-ignition needs a fresh energy injection the model doesn't contain. Splitting and "expelling" orbs requires internal stresses; the network's total cohesion is a few kT per bond, ~10⁻⁴ J for the whole structure (calc 4), with no reservoir to power ejection. At this point the model isn't predicting the phenomenology, it's absorbing it — every reported behavior gets a new clause, and none of the clauses does quantitative work.

On the video, two points, one procedural and one that I think cuts against him sharply. Procedural: a meteor that leaves a dust trail in the troposphere is a deep-penetrating fireball — a sonic-boom, infrasound-detected, all-sky-camera, weather-radar event. The identification "vertical meteor trail" is checkable against AMS/GMN/USG sensor records for that time and place, and if nothing's there, the object is just an unexplained light column in a thunderstorm, which is a crowded reference class. The sharper point: a vertical luminous channel that fragments into five or six blinking beads, in a video that contains a lightning flash, is close to a textbook description of *bead lightning* — the well-documented decay mode in which a lightning channel breaks into a string of luminous segments persisting for seconds. He has, plausibly, filmed the one classical phenomenon whose morphology matches his frame, and bracketed the flash as "likely unrelated." Given the previous discussion, the flash is the most theoretically loaded pixel in the video: lightning through or near a particulate-laden column is the one mechanism on the table that genuinely manufactures compact luminous condensates at the observed positions. If he insists the column is meteor dust, the parsimonious hybrid is that the *lightning* did the concentrating and reducing — at which point the meteor is reduced to optional feedstock, and feedstock is everywhere (his own trail, soil, ash), so the fireball correlation that motivated the whole paper loses its causal role.

What would move me toward his version: the NUFORC video timestamp matching a network-detected fireball with a trajectory overlaying the column; bead spacing along documented persistent trains showing clumping beyond turbulent statistics; or lab demonstration that iron-smoke aggregates in a decaying trail grow past ~mm on minute timescales at realistic n. Each is feasible, none is offered. As it stands, the reply patches coherence and stickiness — which I'd have granted anyway — and leaves mass, luminosity, altitude, and re-ignition each short by orders of magnitude, while introducing a video whose best reading supports the rival mechanism.

## Assistant

This is a graceful concession and the new proposal is the right move within his frame — stratospheric terminal bursts are real, irons do penetrate to 20–30 km, the enlarged mass budget is legitimate, and "sink until heat generation makes you buoyant" is at least self-consistent for large dilute orbs. But the move walks into a bound that I think is the cleanest one yet, because it's nearly thermodynamic: **explosive deposition self-dilutes.**

**The ceiling.** Every kilogram of dust the flare creates arrives with baggage: its ablation heat (~1 MJ/kg for melt spray, 7 MJ/kg for vapor) plus its stopping kinetic energy (½v² ≈ 4.5 MJ/kg at 3 km/s, 110 MJ/kg at 15 km/s), all of which is released into the local air within the dust's centimeter-scale stopping length. At constant pressure that heat inflates the deposit region by ΔV ≈ 0.3·Q/p. So the post-burst dust density has a hard ceiling of roughly ρ_max ~ 3p/(Q_ablate + ½v²) — and the numbers are brutal. At 25 km (p ≈ 2500 Pa) with a fast flare at 15 km/s: ~0.1 g/m³. In the friendliest possible corner — 20 km, body decelerated to the ~3 km/s ablation floor — about 3–4 g/m³. His requirement is 190–950 g/m³ for a 1-m orb, 7–35 g/m³ for a 3-m orb. Note two properties of this bound. It is *mass-independent*: doubling the meteor doubles the dust and doubles the heat, so the 33-kg move buys exactly nothing in concentration — the blast self-regulates. And it *scales with p*: the stratosphere move is backwards. Low ambient pressure is precisely what lets the deposit balloon; the same "much faster diffusion" he cites as aiding coagulation is the flip side of the same low density that dilutes and disperses. Everything that makes the stratosphere gentle makes it dilute.

**The pincer on the friendly corner.** You might notice the 3-m-orb-at-3-km/s corner is only a factor of a few below the ceiling and try to live there. But that corner is pinched from the other side by flare kinematics: fragmenting and ablating cm-scale pieces takes at minimum tens of milliseconds, and 30 ms at 3 km/s is 100 m of path. So the "very short path" is never shorter than ~100–300 m for any body that is still ablating, and 0.5 kg spread over 150 m gives ~3 g per meter of track — grams per orb-sized volume, the same answer the beads-on-a-string picture gave, now derived from his most favorable scenario. Fast flares are killed by energy inflation; slow flares by path length; there's no seam between the horns because the two effects trade off through v in opposite directions.

**The gel threshold makes the shortfall concrete.** Coagulation is local — aggregates grow where they sit, they don't migrate — so the orb can't out-collect its own cubic meter. And there's a lab benchmark for what density an aerosol needs to coagulate into a single connected network rather than flocs: Sorensen and colleagues demonstrated aerosol gelation of carbon nanoparticle smokes at volume fractions of order 10⁻⁴, which for iron means ~1 kg/m³. Below that, the well-documented outcome of a dense metal smoke is micron-to-millimeter flocs that settle out individually. So the required density isn't just Birks's back-of-envelope; it's an empirical phase boundary, sitting two to four orders above the deposition ceiling.

**The descent leg has its own independent problem.** Grant the orb anyway, formed at 20–25 km. Its bulk density there exceeds ambient (0.1–0.5 kg of dust plus 1.2 kg of trapped air in 14 m³, against 0.09 kg/m³ air), so it sinks at ~5 m/s, giving dynamic pressure ~1 Pa on a network whose tensile strength — few-kT bonds at micron mesh spacing — is of order 0.01–0.1 Pa. Shredding margin 10–100×, sustained for the ~hour it takes to descend 20 km, during which it must also cross the jet stream shear layer at 10–12 km and is burning its pyrophoric fuel the whole way, spending the very budget that was supposed to fund an hour of glow at float altitude. And buoyancy arithmetic caps the payload from the other end: lift can't exceed the displaced air deficit, so low-altitude floating pushes him toward large dilute orbs — exactly the ones most easily torn apart.

**Bookkeeping, lightly.** His published statistics required conversion efficiency ~2×10⁻⁸ from the 10-cm class. The ≥20-cm iron class is smaller by roughly an order (size distribution) times ~20 (iron fraction), so supplying the same orb rate now demands efficiency approaching order unity — a seven-order jump in what the mechanism must deliver, requested at the same moment the bounds above show per-step shortfalls of 10²–10⁴. The reframing "we only need 0.3–1.5% capture" sounds modest, but the ceiling says the maximum capture into one connected object is grams over tens of kilograms: ~10⁻⁴, and that's before gelation, descent, and ignition each take their cut.

Where this leaves the physics, I think, is instructive rather than merely negative: the deposit-density ceiling is beaten only by a *long-range* concentrating force that gathers mass after deposition without paying the heat tax — and the only long-range collector operating in the troposphere is the electric field. Charged aerosol in thunderstorm-strength fields drifts and collects; neutral magnetized dust does not (dipole–dipole falls as 1/r⁴ and is dead beyond microns). So even starting from his meteor and conceding every step, the mechanism keeps handing the decisive role back to atmospheric electricity — the third time in this exchange that's happened. If he wants a tractable test before building the detailed formation model, I'd suggest the cheap one: an iron-nanoparticle aerosol chamber at 1–10 g/m³, the most the atmosphere can supply, run against Sorensen's gelation protocol. If it flocs and settles rather than gelling — as the existing literature predicts — the formation question is answered at the bench, no meteors required.
