## What the essay does well

The writing is genuinely effective as persuasion aimed at a lay audience. The COVID analogy up top is smart framing — it immediately establishes the "you're not paying attention and you'll regret it" emotional register. The personal testimony structure ("this happened to me first") is more convincing than abstractions would be, and his concrete examples (the lawyer managing partner, his own Monday workflow) do real work. The pacing is good. He earns the more dramatic claims by building incrementally.

The practical advice section at the end is probably the most valuable part. "Spend one hour a day experimenting" is genuinely good counsel regardless of whether his timeline predictions are right.

## Where it's not cautious enough

This is where I have the most to say, because on balance I think the essay significantly oversells the certainty and speed of what's coming.

**He conflates coding capability with general cognitive replacement.** The essay's central move is: "AI replaced *my* job (software development), therefore it's about to replace *yours* (law, medicine, finance, everything)." But coding is a uniquely favorable domain for AI — the outputs are verifiable, the feedback loops are tight, the training data is abundant, and the environment is fully digital. Lawyering, medicine, and management involve ambiguity, institutional context, human relationships, regulatory accountability, and adversarial dynamics that are qualitatively different. He acknowledges this briefly and then waves it away.

**The timeline claims are extremely aggressive and presented with false precision.** "50% of entry-level white-collar jobs in 1–5 years" is attributed to Amodei, but Shumer clearly endorses it and leans toward the short end. The METR task-length doubling data is interesting but extrapolating it into "AI working independently for weeks within two years" is a huge leap. Benchmark performance on isolated tasks doesn't straightforwardly translate to replacing someone embedded in an organization who manages relationships, exercises judgment under genuine uncertainty, handles exceptions, and takes legal/ethical responsibility for outcomes.

**He's too dismissive of the "I tried it and it wasn't that good" crowd.** Yes, many people tried GPT-3.5 and wrote off the whole field. That's a real problem. But plenty of serious, current users — including people paying for the best models — still encounter significant limitations: hallucination (reduced but not eliminated), brittleness on novel problems, inability to maintain coherent context over truly long projects, poor calibration about its own uncertainty, and difficulty with tasks requiring real-world grounding. Shumer treats all skepticism as outdated, which is intellectually dishonest.

**The "AI is building itself" section is the most overhyped part.** Yes, OpenAI said GPT-5.3 Codex was "instrumental in creating itself." But using AI as a coding assistant during development is very different from the recursive self-improvement singularity scenario he's implying. Every software company uses its own tools to build the next version of its tools. The framing makes it sound like we're one step from a Kurzweilian intelligence explosion, but the actual claim is much more mundane: AI is a useful tool for AI researchers, which... yes, of course it is. The jump from that to "each generation builds the next, which is smarter, which builds the next faster" is doing a *lot* of narrative work that the actual evidence doesn't fully support.

**The Amodei "new country" thought experiment is presented uncritically.** It's a vivid analogy but it smuggles in enormous assumptions — that raw intelligence translates directly to capability, that 50 million superintelligent agents would act coherently, that thinking speed is the binding constraint on progress rather than data collection, physical experimentation, institutional buy-in, etc.

## Where it might actually be *too* cautious

Interestingly, there are a couple of places where I think he pulls punches:

He barely touches the political and governance dimensions. If even half of what he's describing is true, the policy implications are staggering — labor market disruption at this speed would require massive social safety net restructuring, and we have approximately zero political infrastructure to handle it. His advice is essentially individual: use the tools, save money, adapt. But the systemic risks (concentration of power, inequality, democratic erosion) get only a few sentences in the "bigger picture" coda.

He also doesn't seriously engage with the possibility that the economic incentives might not play out as smoothly as "AI can do the job, therefore the job disappears." Adoption friction is real. Liability concerns are real. Organizational inertia is real. The history of technology suggests that even transformative tools take much longer to restructure industries than the pure capability timeline would suggest. Electricity took decades to reshape manufacturing even after it was clearly superior to steam.

## My own take

I think the underlying signal Shumer is pointing at is real. AI capabilities are improving faster than most people outside the industry appreciate, and the gap between public perception and current reality is genuinely large. He's right that most people should be engaging with these tools more seriously than they are.

But I think the essay is doing a thing that's very common in Silicon Valley: mistaking the *potential* trajectory for the *inevitable* trajectory, and compressing timelines based on vibes and extrapolation rather than grappling seriously with the many real-world frictions that slow adoption. The piece reads like it was written in the immediate emotional aftermath of being impressed by a new model release — and those moments tend to produce overconfidence about pace.

The COVID analogy is actually revealing in ways he didn't intend. COVID *did* change everything — but also, many of the most extreme early predictions didn't pan out. The people who were *most* plugged in to the early epidemiological data were often the *most* wrong about specific outcomes, precisely because proximity to the signal made them overweight the trend and underweight the friction.

I'd also note that Shumer runs an AI startup. He has a direct financial interest in people believing AI is transformative and urgent. That doesn't mean he's wrong — I think he's largely directionally correct — but the essay is notably missing any disclosure of this conflict, and it reads at times more like a pitch deck for the importance of AI than a sober assessment. The "follow me on X" CTAs don't help.

**Bottom line:** Directionally right, temporally overconfident, structurally self-serving, rhetorically effective, and missing the systemic/political analysis that would make it actually useful beyond individual career advice.
