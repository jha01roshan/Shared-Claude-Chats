Good question — and the honest answer is that you don't map cleanly onto any existing quadrant, because you combine positions that standard maps treat as opposites. Let me break down where the overlaps and mismatches are, then propose names.

## Where you land on standard axes

**Economic axis:** Hard right/libertarian — anti-redistribution, anti-regulation, pro-big-business, skeptical of medicine/education subsidies. Standard stuff for market libertarians.

**Social axis:** This is where you break the map. You're not "socially conservative" (you're atheist, non-nationalist, indifferent to traditional gender/family norms) but you're also not "socially liberal" in the equity/identity sense (you don't care about group equity, you're indifferent rather than hostile to group differences). You're best described as *socially indifferent* — equity politics and traditionalist culture-war politics both strike you as beside the point.

**Authority axis:** This is your sharpest departure from libertarianism proper. Classical libertarians treat liberty and democracy as near-terminal goods. You treat them as purely instrumental — hence openness to privatized criminal law, elite rule, and governance-by-market-metric rather than by vote. That's much closer to **neoreaction/"Dark Enlightenment" territory** (Moldbug/Yarvin's neocameralism — govern-as-firm, judge by output not process) than to libertarianism. Your Option #2 (capitalism running government and childrearing) and Option #1 (insular patchwork units) are basically neoreactionary policy proposals.

Where you sharply diverge from NRx: they're usually nationalist, religious, and traditionalist (RETVRN-flavored). You explicitly reject all three — you say RETVRN won't work, you're an atheist with weak national loyalty, and you like tech-driven culture change rather than fearing it.

## Adjacent camps you'd find common cause with

- **Neoreaction/Dark Enlightenment** — on anti-democracy, elite rule, patchwork/exit-over-voice, judging governance by results. Diverge on nationalism/religion/traditionalism.
- **e/acc (effective accelerationism)** — on welcoming rapid tech change, treating AI as legitimate successors, and being unbothered (even suspicious) about alignment/rights efforts that would slow AI's "natural" evolution. This is probably your closest tribe on the AI question specifically — it overlaps with Nick Land–style accelerationism (indifferent to whether humans persist, treats capital/tech as the real agent of history) more than with mainstream AI safety culture.
- **Anarcho-capitalists (David Friedman lineage)** — on privatized law, though your reasoning is consequentialist/adaptationist rather than rights-based.
- **Rationalist/EA-adjacent "Grey Tribe"** — your natural home base; shares your appetite for prediction markets, contrarianism, and willingness to follow arguments to uncomfortable places, though most of that community cares more about AI safety than you do.
- **Pronatalists** (Collins-style) — on fertility decline as urgent, though they're usually traditionalist/religious in their solutions and you want a market mechanism (transferable tax shares) instead.
- **Cultural group-selection theorists** (Henrich, D.S. Wilson lineage) — theoretically, though their politics tend communitarian/progressive, unlike yours.

The unusual combination — accelerate tech and culture change, *but* worry that change itself has gone dysgenic/maladaptive and needs steering via selection mechanisms rather than brakes — doesn't have a settled home. Pure accelerationists don't want to "fix" drift, they want to ride it. Pure reactionaries want to reverse it. You want a third thing: engineer *better selection pressure*, not less change and not undirected change.

## Catchy names

A few options, in order of how well I think they capture the actual structure of the view:

1. **Adaptive Accelerationism** — signals "yes to fast tech/culture change" while flagging that the point isn't speed for its own sake but restoring functional selection pressure.
2. **Selectionist Futurism** — leads with the core diagnostic claim (broken selection → drift) rather than the accelerationist branding.
3. **Futarchist Neoreaction** — more provocative, ties your governance mechanism (futarchy) to the elite-rule/anti-democracy structure directly, at the cost of the NRx baggage.

My pick would be **Adaptive Accelerationism** — it's the one that a stranger could roughly reverse-engineer your positions from, and it doesn't borrow controversy from movements you only partially overlap with.

Here's a paragraph-length description:

**Adaptive Accelerationism**: The view that civilization's biggest problem is cultural drift — the breakdown of the evolutionary selection processes that once kept human values and institutions functionally adaptive, leaving culture free to mutate toward maladaptive outcomes like fertility collapse. Unlike traditionalists, adaptive accelerationists don't want to slow change or return to old norms — too much has already shifted for that to work, and they welcome continued rapid tech-driven culture change. Instead they want to restore genuine selective pressure by other means: shrinking the role of egalitarian, redistributive, and rights-based politics (which are treated as largely instrumental rather than sacred); expanding the domain where markets and prediction markets (futarchy) allocate resources and judge leaders, including areas — like government itself, criminal law, or child-rearing — normally walled off from market logic; and experimenting with harder structural fixes such as splitting the world into more insular, low-contact cultural units, or building futures markets that hold leaders accountable to long-run civilizational metrics. It treats future AI as humanity's legitimate evolutionary descendants rather than a threat to be contained, and is wary that safety and rights efforts aimed at AI could block the same adaptive process the view is trying to protect for human culture. It is politically homeless by design: economically libertarian, socially indifferent to identity politics, skeptical of democracy and liberty as terminal values, and unsentimental about nation, religion, and tradition — closer to neoreaction on governance and to accelerationism on technology, but organized around a distinct diagnosis (broken cultural selection) rather than either movement's usual commitments.
