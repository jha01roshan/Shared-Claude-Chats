## The Book of Jude — A Study Overview

**Author:** Jude (Greek: Ἰούδας, *Ioudas*), who identifies himself as "a servant of Jesus Christ and a brother of James" (verse 1). This makes him a half-brother of Jesus himself — yet rather than claiming that family connection, he humbly calls himself a δοῦλος (*doulos*, G1401), a servant or slave. His brother James did the same in his own letter (James 1:1).

**Greek opening:**
> Ἰούδας Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ δοῦλος, ἀδελφὸς δὲ Ἰακώβου, τοῖς ἐν θεῷ πατρὶ ἠγαπημένοις καὶ Ἰησοῦ Χριστῷ τετηρημένοις κλητοῖς

Notice the beautiful theology packed into the greeting: Jude's readers are described as ἠγαπημένοις (*ēgapēmenois*, "loved" — perfect passive, meaning they have been and remain loved by God the Father) and τετηρημένοις (*tetērēmenois*, "kept" — also perfect passive, meaning they are continually preserved in Jesus Christ). The theme of being *kept* by God bookends the entire letter (compare verse 24).

**Audience:** We don't know exactly who Jude's readers were, but they were a community of believers facing a crisis: false teachers had infiltrated their fellowship.

---

### Purpose and Occasion

Jude originally planned to write about the salvation he shared with his readers, but he felt compelled to write urgently about a different matter — people who had secretly slipped into the community and were distorting God's grace into a license for immoral behavior (verses 3–4). His purpose is twofold: to warn against these false teachers and to encourage believers to contend for the faith.

---

### Structure of the Letter

The letter follows a clear pattern:

**Verses 1–2** — Greeting and blessing (mercy, peace, and love)

**Verses 3–4** — The occasion: contend for the faith, because false teachers have crept in

**Verses 5–16** — Three rounds of Old Testament examples paired with applications, using a Jewish *midrash* style:

| Example | Application |
|---|---|
| vv. 5–7 (Israel in the wilderness, fallen angels, Sodom & Gomorrah) | v. 8 |
| v. 9 (Michael and the devil over Moses' body) | v. 10 |
| v. 11 (Cain, Balaam, Korah) | vv. 12–13 |
| vv. 14–15 (Enoch's prophecy) | v. 16 |
| vv. 17–18 (the apostles' warning) | v. 19 |

**Verses 20–23** — Practical exhortation: build yourselves up, pray, keep yourselves in God's love, show mercy to the wavering

**Verses 24–25** — One of the most beloved doxologies in all of Scripture

---

### Key People Referenced

Jude draws on a remarkably wide cast of characters: **Adam, Enoch, Cain, Balaam, Korah, Moses, Michael the archangel, James, and Jesus**. He also references the people of **Sodom, Gomorrah, and Egypt**, plus fallen angels. What's especially fascinating is that Jude quotes from extrabiblical Jewish literature — he draws from *1 Enoch* (verses 14–15) and alludes to the *Assumption of Moses* (verse 9), using these traditions as his Jewish audience would have recognized them.

---

### Key Themes to Watch For

**Being "kept"** — The Greek verb τηρέω (*tēreō*) appears repeatedly. God *keeps* believers (v. 1), fallen angels are *kept* in chains for judgment (v. 6), darkness is *kept* for false teachers (v. 13), and believers are urged to *keep* themselves in God's love (v. 21). God is the ultimate keeper (v. 24).

**Contending for the faith** — "The faith" (τῇ πίστει, v. 3) refers not to the personal act of believing but to the body of Christian teaching, delivered once for all to the saints.

**Nature imagery** — Jude is a vivid writer. False teachers are waterless clouds, fruitless autumn trees doubly dead, wild waves foaming up their shame, and wandering stars destined for darkness (vv. 12–13).

**Grace and judgment** — The letter holds both in tension. God's grace must not be twisted into license for sin, but believers are also called to show mercy even to those being misled.

## Jude 1:7 — Verse Study

**Greek text:**
> ὡς Σόδομα καὶ Γόμορρα καὶ αἱ περὶ αὐτὰς πόλεις τὸν ὅμοιον τρόπον τούτοις ἐκπορνεύσασαι καὶ ἀπελθοῦσαι ὀπίσω σαρκὸς ἑτέρας, πρόκεινται δεῖγμα πυρὸς αἰωνίου δίκην ὑπέχουσαι.

**Translation:** "Just as Sodom and Gomorrah, and the cities around them, having in a similar manner to these indulged in sexual immorality and gone after strange flesh, are set forth as an example, undergoing the penalty of eternal fire."

---

### Context in Jude's Argument

This verse is the **third example** in a triad of divine judgments (verses 5–7). Jude is building a case that God has always punished rebellion and unfaithfulness:

1. **v. 5** — Israel delivered from Egypt, then destroyed for unbelief
2. **v. 6** — Angels who abandoned their proper dwelling, now kept in chains
3. **v. 7** — Sodom, Gomorrah, and surrounding cities, destroyed by fire

Each example escalates. And the application follows in verse 8: the false teachers in the community are doing the very same kinds of things — defiling their bodies, rejecting authority, and blaspheming spiritual beings.

---

### Key Greek Words

**ἐκπορνεύσασαι** (*ekporneusasai*, G1608) — This intensified form of πορνεύω (*porneuō*, "to commit sexual immorality") appears only here in the New Testament. The prefix ἐκ- intensifies the verb, suggesting an extreme or excessive degree of sexual sin. The aorist participle indicates a decisive, completed action — they gave themselves over to it fully. It's parsed as V-AAP-NPF (aorist active participle, nominative plural feminine), agreeing grammatically with "cities" (πόλεις), which in Greek is feminine.

**ἀπελθοῦσαι ὀπίσω σαρκὸς ἑτέρας** — "having gone after strange/other flesh." This is a critical phrase:

- **ἀπελθοῦσαι ὀπίσω** (*apelthousai opisō*) literally means "having gone away after" — a biblical idiom for actively pursuing something forbidden. This same construction is used throughout the Old Testament (in the Septuagint) to describe Israel "going after" foreign gods.

- **σαρκὸς ἑτέρας** (*sarkos heteras*, G4561 + G2087) — "other/different flesh." The adjective ἑτέρας (*heteras*) doesn't just mean "other" in the sense of "another of the same kind" (that would be ἄλλος, *allos*). Rather, ἕτερος implies *different in kind*. The flesh being pursued is of a fundamentally different category or nature. The study notes indicate this likely refers to the people of Sodom pursuing sexual relations that crossed a boundary of kind — most scholars connect this to the account in Genesis 19:5–10.

**τὸν ὅμοιον τρόπον τούτοις** — "in a similar manner to these." The word τούτοις (*toutois*, "these") likely refers back to the angels in verse 6. The connection Jude is drawing is that just as the angels in verse 6 crossed a boundary between the heavenly and earthly realms (drawing on the tradition of Genesis 6:1–4, where "sons of God" came to "daughters of men"), so the people of Sodom crossed a boundary of flesh — pursuing what was not proper to them. Both examples involve transgression of divinely established categories.

**πρόκεινται δεῖγμα** (*prokeintai deigma*) — "are set forth as an example." The verb πρόκειμαι (*prokeimai*, G4295) means "to lie before, to be publicly displayed." The present tense is significant — Jude says these cities *are* (not "were") set forth. Their destruction remains a visible, ongoing lesson. The word δεῖγμα (*deigma*, G1164) means "a specimen, a sample, an example for warning." It appears only here in the New Testament.

**πυρὸς αἰωνίου δίκην ὑπέχουσαι** — "undergoing the penalty of eternal fire."

- **δίκην ὑπέχουσαι** (*dikēn hypechousai*): δίκη (*dikē*, G1349) means "justice, penalty, punishment." The verb ὑπέχω (*hypechō*, G5254) means "to undergo, to suffer." Together they form a legal expression: "suffering the just penalty."
- **πυρὸς αἰωνίου** (*pyros aiōniou*, G4442 + G166): "of eternal fire." This phrase is debated — does it mean the fire itself is eternal, or that the consequences are eternal? The physical cities were destroyed by literal fire (Genesis 19:24–25), but they are not still burning. So "eternal fire" likely points beyond the historical event to the eschatological judgment that the destruction foreshadows. Peter's parallel in 2 Peter 2:6 makes this connection explicit, saying God condemned the cities as "an example of what is coming upon the ungodly" (ὑπόδειγμα μελλόντων ἀσεβέσιν).

---

### The Parallel with 2 Peter 2:6

Jude and 2 Peter share much of the same material. Compare:

- **Jude 7**: Sodom and Gomorrah πρόκεινται δεῖγμα — "are set forth as an example"
- **2 Peter 2:6**: ὑπόδειγμα μελλόντων ἀσεβέσιν τεθεικώς — "having set them as an example of what is coming upon the ungodly"

Both writers use the Sodom story as a warning pattern, but Peter makes the forward-looking dimension more explicit. The destruction that already happened points toward a future judgment.

---

### Why This Matters in Jude's Letter

Jude isn't simply retelling old stories. He's building a theological argument: **God's judgment is real, it is patterned, and it is coming for those who follow the same path.** The false teachers who have crept into the community (v. 4) are characterized by the same sins — they "defile the flesh" (v. 8), just as Sodom indulged in sexual immorality. They "reject authority" (v. 8), just as the angels abandoned their proper place. They "blaspheme glorious ones" (v. 8), showing contempt for the spiritual realm.

The examples in verses 5–7 aren't historical curiosities. They are *precedents* — divine courtroom evidence that the same Judge will render the same verdict on the same kind of sin.

## Jude 1:9 — Verse Study

**Greek text:**
> ὁ δὲ Μιχαὴλ ὁ ἀρχάγγελος, ὅτε τῷ διαβόλῳ διακρινόμενος διελέγετο περὶ τοῦ Μωϋσέως σώματος, οὐκ ἐτόλμησεν κρίσιν ἐπενεγκεῖν βλασφημίας ἀλλ᾽ εἶπεν· ἐπιτιμήσαι σοι κύριος.

**Translation:** "But Michael the archangel, when he was disputing with the devil and arguing about the body of Moses, did not dare to bring a slanderous judgment against him, but said, 'The Lord rebuke you!'"

---

### The Big Question: Where Does This Story Come From?

This is the verse that stops almost every reader in their tracks. The Old Testament tells us that Moses died in Moab and that God buried him:

**Deuteronomy 34:5–6** (Hebrew):
> וַיָּמָת שָׁם מֹשֶׁה עֶבֶד־יְהוָה בְּאֶרֶץ מוֹאָב עַל־פִּי יְהוָה׃ וַיִּקְבֹּר אֹתוֹ בַגַּיְ בְּאֶרֶץ מוֹאָב מוּל בֵּית פְּעוֹר וְלֹא־יָדַע אִישׁ אֶת־קְבֻרָתוֹ עַד הַיּוֹם הַזֶּה׃

"And Moses the servant of Yahweh died there in the land of Moab, according to the mouth of Yahweh. And he buried him in the valley in the land of Moab, opposite Beth-peor, and no one knows his burial place to this day."

Notice the mystery embedded in the text — God himself buried Moses, and *no one knows where.* That unusual detail became a seedbed for Jewish tradition. The work known as the **Assumption (or Testament) of Moses** — a Jewish writing from roughly the first century — apparently told the story of Satan disputing with Michael over Moses' body. We don't have a complete copy of this text; it has been reconstructed from fragments and references by other ancient writers (including this very verse in Jude).

What was the dispute about? The traditions vary, but the most common explanation is that **the devil was claiming a right to Moses' body**, possibly on the grounds that Moses had committed murder (Exodus 2:12) and was therefore under the devil's jurisdiction. Michael, rather than pronouncing his own judgment on the devil, deferred to the Lord.

---

### Key Greek Words

**Μιχαὴλ ὁ ἀρχάγγελος** (*Michaēl ho archangelos*, G3413 + G743) — "Michael the archangel." The name מִיכָאֵל (*Mîḵāʾēl*) in Hebrew means "Who is like God?" — itself a rhetorical declaration of God's incomparability. The word ἀρχάγγελος (*archangelos*) appears only twice in the New Testament — here and in 1 Thessalonians 4:16. It combines ἀρχή (*archē*, "chief, first") with ἄγγελος (*angelos*, "messenger/angel"). Michael appears in Daniel 10:13 and 10:21 as one of the chief princes who fights on Israel's behalf, in Daniel 12:1 as the great prince who stands guard over God's people, and in Revelation 12:7 leading the heavenly armies against the dragon.

**τῷ διαβόλῳ** (*tō diabolō*, G1228) — "the devil." The word διάβολος literally means "slanderer, accuser" — one who throws charges across (*dia-* + *ballō*). This is the Greek equivalent of the Hebrew שָׂטָן (*śāṭān*, "the accuser/adversary"). It's significant that the devil here is doing what he always does: *accusing*. He is making a legal claim against Moses.

**διακρινόμενος διελέγετο** (*diakrinomenos dielegeto*, G1252 + G1256) — Two words stacked together to convey the intensity of this confrontation. διακρίνω (*diakrinō*) means "to contend, to dispute, to separate by distinguishing." διαλέγομαι (*dialegomai*) means "to reason, to argue, to debate" — it's where we get "dialogue" and "dialectic." The imperfect tense (διελέγετο) pictures an ongoing, extended argument — this wasn't a brief exchange.

**οὐκ ἐτόλμησεν** (*ouk etolmēsen*, G5111) — "did not dare." This is the crux of the verse's argument. The aorist active ἐτόλμησεν (*etolmēsen*) from τολμάω (*tolmaō*) means "to have the audacity, to presume, to venture." Even **Michael the archangel** — the highest-ranking angel, the commander of heaven's armies — *did not dare* to pronounce judgment on his own authority against the devil. If the mightiest created being recognized such restraint, how much more audacious are the false teachers who casually blaspheme spiritual authorities (verse 8)?

**κρίσιν ἐπενεγκεῖν βλασφημίας** (*krisin epenegkein blasphēmias*) — "to bring a judgment of slander/blasphemy." This is a legal phrase. κρίσις (*krisis*, G2920) is "judgment, verdict." ἐπιφέρω (*epipherō*, G2018) means "to bring upon, to pronounce against." βλασφημία (*blasphēmia*, G988) here doesn't mean blasphemy against God, but rather a *slanderous accusation* — a reviling condemnation. Michael refused to fight the devil with the devil's own weapon (slander).

**ἐπιτιμήσαι σοι κύριος** (*epitimēsai soi kyrios*) — "The Lord rebuke you!" This is an optative form (ἐπιτιμήσαι, V-AAO-3S), expressing a wish or prayer: "May the Lord rebuke you." Michael handed the matter over to divine authority rather than acting on his own.

---

### The Zechariah 3 Connection

Michael's words are a near-exact echo of **Zechariah 3:2**, where the angel of Yahweh says to Satan:

> וַיֹּאמֶר יְהוָה אֶל־הַשָּׂטָן **יִגְעַר יְהוָה בְּךָ** הַשָּׂטָן

"And Yahweh said to the accuser: **May Yahweh rebuke you**, O accuser!"

In Zechariah's vision, Satan is standing as an accuser against Joshua the high priest, who stands before the angel of the Lord in filthy garments. The same pattern appears: the accuser brings charges, and rather than engaging the accuser on his terms, the response is to invoke the Lord's authority. The parallel is almost certainly deliberate — Jude (or the tradition he draws from) is linking Michael's encounter with this prophetic template.

---

### What Jude's Argument Is Doing

This is the second "example–application" pair in verses 8–10. The structure is:

- **Verse 8**: The false teachers "reject authority and blaspheme the glorious ones" (δόξας βλασφημοῦσιν)
- **Verse 9**: *But even Michael*, the most powerful angel, didn't presume to pronounce a slanderous judgment against the devil himself
- **Verse 10**: These false teachers, by contrast, blaspheme "whatever they do not understand"

The argument is devastating in its logic. It works from greater to lesser: If the *archangel* showed such restraint before the *devil*, who are these false teachers to hurl accusations and insults against spiritual beings they don't even understand? Their arrogance is breathtaking precisely because it exceeds even what the highest angel would attempt.

---

### The Question of Extrabiblical Sources

This is the second place where Jude draws on Jewish literature outside the Old Testament canon (the first being the allusion to Genesis 6/1 Enoch traditions in verse 6, and the explicit Enoch quotation comes in verses 14–15). Some readers find this unsettling, but a few observations are helpful here. Jude isn't saying the *Assumption of Moses* is Scripture. He's using a story his audience knew and recognized — much as a preacher today might reference a well-known illustration. The theological point he makes from the story is consistent with the rest of Scripture: God is the ultimate judge, and even the highest spiritual beings defer to his authority. Paul similarly quotes pagan poets in Acts 17:28 and Titus 1:12 without endorsing their entire body of work.

---

### Summary

Verse 9 is a masterclass in argumentation. Jude takes a vivid story his audience would have known, featuring the most exalted angel facing the most evil adversary, over the body of the most revered leader in Israel's history — and shows that even in that extraordinary situation, Michael deferred to God rather than presuming to pronounce judgment himself. The false teachers' casual blasphemy of spiritual realities they don't understand is exposed as absurd and dangerous.
