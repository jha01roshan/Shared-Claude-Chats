Here's how South Africa compares to the world:

**South Africa** holds the grim distinction of being the most unequal country on Earth. South Africa leads the global ranking with a Gini index of **0.63**. The richest 10% of South Africans hold 71% of the wealth, while the poorest 60% hold just 7%. This extreme inequality is largely rooted in history — economists attribute it to historical land ownership laws, the lingering socio-economic impacts of apartheid, and an economy heavily reliant on undiversified natural resource extraction.

**The World (Global Gini)** is trickier to pin down, because it measures inequality across all of humanity rather than within a single country. Different scholars estimate the global Gini index to range between **0.61 and 0.68**. Interestingly, when measured this way — treating every person on Earth as part of one big "country" — global inequality ends up being comparable to South Africa's, because the gap between the world's richest and poorest nations is enormous.

If you're comparing South Africa to a simple **global average of national Gini scores**, the picture looks different. The World Bank reports a global mean Gini of approximately **0.38** for disposable income, meaning South Africa's 0.63 is dramatically higher than what most countries experience — nearly double the average of low-inequality regions like the Nordic countries (around 0.25–0.28).

**In short:** South Africa is the world's most unequal country by national Gini, with a score of ~0.63 — far above the global average of ~0.38 for individual nations. The only context in which South Africa looks "typical" is when compared to global inequality measured across all of humanity at once, which happens to be similarly high due to vast income differences between rich and poor nations.
