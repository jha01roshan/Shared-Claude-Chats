The Satir Change Model — often drawn as a "J-curve" — comes from family therapist Virginia Satir, who noticed that people and groups follow a predictable emotional and performance arc whenever a significant change hits. The key insight is that things reliably get *worse* before they get better: performance dips into a slump before recovering to a new, usually higher, level. Knowing the shape in advance is what stops people from abandoning a change while they're still in the dip.

Walking through the five stages:

Late status quo is the familiar starting point. Everything is predictable, roles and routines are known, and performance is stable. It isn't necessarily *good* — it's just comfortable, and problems may be quietly accumulating underneath. People feel a sense of belonging here precisely because they know what to expect.

The foreign element is the trigger: a new manager, a reorganisation, new software, a piece of unwelcome feedback, a life event. It violates an expectation and can't be ignored. The first reaction is usually resistance — denial, blame, trying to carry on as before, or arguing that the change isn't needed. The aim of good change leadership at this point is to help people stay open rather than dig in.

Chaos is the heart of the model and the bottom of the J. The old ways no longer work and the new ways aren't mastered yet, so performance drops and anxiety rises. People feel disoriented, may revert to old habits, and behave erratically. This is the most important thing to understand: the dip is normal and expected, not a sign that the change has failed. Most failed changes are abandoned here, because people mistake the slump for proof that the new approach is wrong.

The transforming idea is the turning point — a new insight, a reframe, or a moment where the change suddenly makes sense ("oh, *that's* how this works"). It gives people a path out of chaos. From there, integration is the practising phase: people try the new approach, build competence, and performance climbs as the new way becomes more natural. It still takes effort and support, but the trajectory is now upward.

Finally, the new status quo settles in at a new level of stability, typically higher than where things started. The change has become the new normal, and the cycle is ready to begin again when the next foreign element arrives.

A few practical takeaways that make the model useful rather than just descriptive. Name the dip in advance, so when chaos arrives people recognise it as a stage rather than a disaster. Expect a temporary performance and morale drop and resource for it, instead of punishing it. Invest heavily in support during chaos — coaching, reassurance, quick wins, and clear information all shorten the trough and surface the transforming idea sooner. And resist the urge to retreat to the old status quo when things feel worst, because that abandons the change at the exact point just before recovery would have begun.

The model shows up well beyond therapy — it's widely used in organisational change management, agile and software teams adopting new tools or processes, and personal transitions like a new job or learning a hard skill. The same shape applies: a stable start, a disruption, a messy middle, a breakthrough, and a higher new normal.

Good addition — Lewin's model is the older, more foundational of the two, and the two map onto each other neatly. Kurt Lewin, a social psychologist writing in the 1940s, described change as a three-stage process using a metaphor of reshaping a block of ice: you can't turn a cube into a cone by carving it, you have to melt it, pour it into a new mould, and let it set again.

The three stages in brief: unfreeze means loosening the current equilibrium — building the case for change, raising awareness of why the old way no longer works, and overcoming the natural pull toward "we've always done it this way." Change (sometimes called the moving or transition phase) is the period of actually adopting new processes and behaviours, marked by uncertainty as people learn. Refreeze means stabilising the new state so it becomes the default — reinforcing the new behaviours through habits, structures, rewards, and norms so the organisation doesn't slide back.

The two models describe the same journey at different resolutions, which is why they're often taught together. Lewin gives you the macro sequence — you must destabilise before you can move, and re-stabilise after — while Satir zooms into Lewin's middle "change" stage and tells you what it actually *feels* like from the inside. Roughly, Lewin's unfreeze corresponds to Satir's foreign element and resistance; Lewin's change phase contains Satir's whole chaos → transforming idea → integration arc, including the all-important performance dip; and Lewin's refreeze is Satir's new status quo.

That difference in resolution is the practical reason to know both. Lewin's model is clean and memorable but can make change sound tidier than it is — its "change" box hides the messy slump. Satir's J-curve fills that gap by naming the chaos and warning you the dip is coming, so leaders don't panic or reverse course at the worst moment. Used together, Lewin tells you the order of operations and Satir tells you to brace for the valley in the middle.

One modern caveat worth noting: Lewin's "refreeze" stage has drawn criticism in fast-moving environments, where some argue you should never fully refreeze because the organisation needs to stay permanently adaptable rather than settling into a new rigid state. Critics also point out that Lewin's model is more of a planned, top-down framework, which fits deliberate transformations better than the continuous, emergent change many organisations now face.
