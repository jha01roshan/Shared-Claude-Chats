## Assistant

Yeah, this is a real and underappreciated distinction. There are people who route *around* problems, and people who route *through* them. Both groups get their immediate task done, but the second group leaves the path cleared for everyone who comes after.

It's deeper than "teach a man to fish" though, because that framing is about self-sufficiency — learning the skill so *you* don't need help again. What you're describing is more prosocial than that. The fixer doesn't just learn to fish; they figure out why the river dried up, fix the dam, and tell the village. They absorb a cost that isn't strictly theirs to bear.

There's something almost infrastructural about it. The workaround-only people are consumers of a functioning world. The fixers are *maintainers* of it. And the cruel irony is that when fixers do their job well, it's invisible — the problem just quietly stops existing, and nobody knows it was ever there.

A few layers to why this dynamic persists the way it does:

The **cost asymmetry** is real. The workaround takes 2 minutes. Diagnosing the root cause, fixing it, testing it, and writing it up might take 2 hours. So in any purely local cost-benefit calculation, the workaround wins. The fixer is doing something economically irrational *for themselves* — it only makes sense at the collective level.

There's also a **temperament** thing. Fixers tend to have a specific kind of itch — an unresolved root cause feels like an open wound to them. They're not being heroic; they literally can't leave it alone. The workaround feels *wrong*, like putting tape over a check engine light.

And then there's the **reporting back** part, which is actually the rarest piece. Plenty of people will quietly fix something for themselves and move on. The ones who document it and share the fix — that's a third, even smaller group. That's the village-level thinking.

It maps nicely onto open source culture, where this exact gradient is visible: most people use the software, some file bug reports, fewer submit patches, and the rarest write up what they found so others can learn from it.

Okay, linguistic groundwork. When English creates words for "a concept applied to something," there are a few well-worn patterns:

**Verb from noun/concept** — the simplest. You just declare the concept a verb. Google → "google it." Jury-rig → "jury-rigged." Bootstrap → "bootstrapped." The bridge was broken and they *bootstrapped* it. This is how most modern coinages work. Just brute force a noun into verb position and let usage do the rest.

**Latin/Greek root construction** — for when you want it to sound like it belongs in a textbook. You'd combine roots that encode meaning. *Pro* (forward) + *altru* (other) + some suffix. *Synergate.* *Propacure* (propagate + cure). This gives you academic weight but can feel sterile.

**Germanic compound** — English loves smashing two concrete words together. Drawbridge. Foothold. Feedback. Upstream. These feel *old* and *solid.* "Trailseed." "Fixwake." "Pathgraft." The bridge was broken and they *pathgrafted* it.

**The -ify / -ate suffix** — turns a concept into a process. Clarify. Propagate. Cultivate. Solidify. So: "The bridge was broken and they *upliftened* it" — no. "They *synergated* it" — closer. "They *resolveforwarded* it" — too clunky.

**The blend/portmanteau** — smash two words and keep the best parts. Smoke + fog = smog. Stay + vacation = staycation. This is where the magic usually lives.

So the real question for coining yours is: what are the **two or three core feelings** compressed into this word? Because the best coinages are basically zip files of meaning.
