This is a fascinating connection! The Protocol Reader actually *does* make this link explicitly, though briefly. In "The Unreasonable Sufficiency of Protocols," Venkatesh and co-authors write: "Good protocols tend to form persistent Schelling points in spaces of problems worth solving, around solutions good enough to live with---for a while."

But you're probing something deeper about *sacredness specifically* as a type of Schelling point, which hasn't been systematically developed. Here's what I found:

## The Structural Similarities

Both sacredness and Schelling points involve:

**1. Salience through distinction** 
- Schelling points work because something "stands out" - the red square among blue squares, Grand Central at noon
- Sacred things are literally "set apart" from the profane/ordinary, creating the same kind of perceptual prominence

**2. Coordination without communication**
- Schelling points emerge through recursive mutual modeling: "I think you'll think I'll think..." 
- Sacred things create convergence through shared reverence without explicit negotiation about what's holy

**3. Cultural dependency**
- Schelling points depend entirely on shared context - Grand Central only works if you both know NYC
- Sacredness is, per Durkheim, socially constructed to reflect "collective consciousness"

**4. Self-reinforcing dynamics**
- The more people coordinate on a Schelling point, the stronger it becomes
- The more people treat something as sacred, the more sacred it becomes through ritual reinforcement

**5. Resistance to rational calculation**
- Schelling himself noted you can't computationally derive focal points - it's like determining if a joke is funny
- Sacred things resist cost-benefit analysis; violating them feels categorically wrong, not just inefficient

## What Makes Sacredness Special

But sacredness seems to be a *particularly strong* type of Schelling point:

**Enforcement intensity**: The Protocol Reader discusses sacredness in contexts like "the archive that only grows and its concurrent religiosity" and notes how certain protocols create "the 'majesty protocol' because it operates to uphold respect and veneration." Sacred Schelling points come with taboos, moral weight, even violence for violations - much stronger than regular coordination failures.

**Axis mundi function**: Eliade's work on the sacred emphasizes how sacred things provide *orientation* - they establish "a fixed point in what was previously an infinite expanse." This is exactly what Schelling points do for coordination problems, but sacred things do it for *existence itself*.

**Time dimension**: Sacred time is "reversible and eternally present" through ritual reenactment. Regular Schelling points don't have this temporal persistence mechanism.

## Potential Contribution Areas

1. **Sacred things as ultimate Schelling points for social order** - How do societies use sacredness to solve coordination problems that regular protocols can't handle? Life-safety protocols creating strong moral commitments would be one example.

2. **Enforcement economics of sacred vs. profane Schelling points** - What's the cost structure difference? Sacred violations trigger moral outrage (cheap, distributed enforcement) vs. regular coordination failure (requires costly monitoring).

3. **The ritual-protocol connection** - Ritual as the mechanism that *maintains* sacred Schelling points across time. Durkheim saw ritual as reaffirming collective consciousness; in protocol terms, it's consensus maintenance.

4. **Boundary work** - How sacredness creates harder boundaries than other Schelling points. The sacred/profane distinction is more rigid than most focal point distinctions.

5. **Pollution dynamics** - Your work on spheres of exchange connects here. Sacred things contaminate profane things, creating coordination around what *not* to mix - a negative Schelling point.
