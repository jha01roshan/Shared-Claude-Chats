The UBI critique you raise is particularly sharp. If we're essentially having companies fund UBI through taxes, which then gets spent back at those same companies, it does seem circular. Why not just... give people the goods directly?

Here's something I'm curious about: In your scenario where AI is better at "most" jobs - what happens to the jobs where humans still have an edge? Do you think there are roles that will remain fundamentally human, and could those become highly valued in unexpected ways?

## Assistant

Exactly right - the Ford analogy breaks down when you scale it up. If Ford workers were the *only* customers, it would be a closed loop that makes no economic sense. Ford succeeded because his workers were just one part of a broader economy with many employers and consumers.

So in an AI-dominated economy, if most companies are essentially funding UBI to create their own customer base, where does the actual value creation happen? It seems like we'd just be shuffling money in circles.

This makes me wonder: could the solution involve fundamentally changing who *owns* the AI systems? Right now we're assuming companies own the AI workers and then somehow need to pay humans to buy from them. But what if the humans owned shares in the AI productivity directly?

Like, instead of "Acme Corp owns AI that makes widgets, pays taxes for UBI, humans use UBI to buy widgets," what if it's "humans collectively own AI that makes widgets, and directly benefit from the productivity"?

But even then, I'm struggling with the mechanism. If everyone owns everything, who decides what gets produced and how much? Markets work because of scarcity and different preferences, but if AI can make anything cheaply and everyone owns the means of production...

Do you think we're hitting up against the limits of market-based economics entirely? Or is there some way supply and demand could still function?
