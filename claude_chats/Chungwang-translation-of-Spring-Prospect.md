**Nomenclature first**: it's *Chūn wàng* 春望 (Wade–Giles *Ch'un-wang*). "Chungwang" is a different syllable — 中/重/仲.

## The main objection

The four compressions optimise **character count**. Character count is the one formal property of a 五言律詩 that carries no meaning of its own; it's a by-product of the constraints that *do* carry meaning. The premise behind the ladder — that a 5-character line is dense because it is *short*, and that shortening English is therefore an approach to it — is Fenollosa's error, the one Pound built the ideogrammic method on. It was demolished long ago (Kennedy 1958; Saussy's 2008 edition of Fenollosa) and defended in a subtler form by Yip Wai-lim. Du Fu's lines are not fragments. They are complete, grammatical, urbane sentences in the most conventional and most regulated form available to him. Telegraphic English is a *marked* register — Imagism, headlinese, war telegram — and it imports rupture and modernity that aren't in the Chinese. Compressing makes Du Fu sound avant-garde. He is the opposite of avant-garde; that is the point of him.

## The structural point, which is the whole thing

The rhyme is 深 / 心 / 金 / 簪 — all in the 十二侵 group, all *-im* in Middle Chinese, a tight monorhyme on the even lines. But the load-bearing feature is parallelism, and Du Fu overdoes it: couplets 1, 2 *and* 3 are all 對句, where the form only obliges 2 and 3. Three couplets of rigid, impersonal, verb-matched symmetry — and then the closing couplet breaks it and *runs*:

> 白頭搔更短，渾欲不勝簪。

Symmetry, symmetry, symmetry, collapse. The poem's shape is an argument: the world holds its form (mountains, rivers, spring, grass) while the man in it comes apart. The final couplet is the only continuous, personal, temporal gesture in the poem — scratch → sparser → soon the pin won't hold → I am aging out of the office I still nominally serve. The whole poem funnels from empire → capital → flowers and birds → war → family → self → white head → a single hairpin. That funnel is deictic and syntactic. It needs syntax.

Now look at what your ladder does. Line 1 compresses beautifully — "State; hills." is genuinely good, because that line *is* juxtapositive. Line 8 dies: "No hold." is inert; "Hair thins. / No hold." is nothing. That asymmetry is the experiment's actual finding, and it refutes the experiment's premise. The poem is not uniformly ideogrammic. Its best moment is the one place where the form stops being ideogrammic, and compression is precisely the operation that cannot render it.

## Versions 2–5 are not translations

They're compressions of version 1. The tell: every one inherits "weeds" ("Grass buries the old", "Town overgrown", "Town wilding", "Green ruins"), every one inherits "startle", and every one resolves the poem's central ambiguity the same way. Anyone going back to the Chinese four times would have varied at least the last of those. So they can't be assessed as translations; they're a stylistic exercise on an English text — which is Weinberger's *Nineteen Ways of Looking at Wang Wei*, and worth reading if you haven't, because he does the comparison you're implicitly attempting.

## Line-level

- **山河 → "the mountains"**: drops 河 and breaks a fixed binome. 國 (polity) vs 山河 (the land's body) is the antithesis the entire poem hangs from. Half of it is not enough.
- **草木深 → "deep in the weeds"**: two problems. 草木 is neutral vegetation, and the horror is that it is *lush*, not that it is *weedy* — spring's fecundity indifferent to the catastrophe. "Weeds" moralises it. Worse, "deep in the weeds" is a live idiom meaning "bogged down in minutiae." That's a register collision, and a fatal one.
- **感時花濺淚**: you've silently committed to the minority reading (Yoshikawa Kōjirō's) in which the flowers themselves weep. Both readings are current and Chinese commentators split, but the dominant one has 花 and 鳥 as *stimuli*, not agents: moved by the times, he spatters tears at flowers; sick of parting, birds jolt his heart. That reading also preserves the parallel more cleanly (感時 / 恨別 are both his states; 花 / 鳥 both external). The pathetic-fallacy reading turns Du Fu into a sentimentalist. It's a choice, and it isn't flagged.
- **濺 → "wear"**: 濺 is *spatter, spurt* — violent. "Wear tears" is decorative jewellery.
- **恨別 → "Bereft"**: 別 is separation from a living family (his wife and children at Qiang village). "Bereft" implies death.
- **萬金 → "thousands in gold"**: 萬 is the conventional myriad. You've downgraded a fixed hyperbole for the metre.
- **短 → "thinner"**: this one I'll concede — 短 has long been used for hair thinning, so it's defensible. But "each time" is an iterative you've added; 更 is just "even more."
- **The final enjambment**: lüshi lines are end-stopped. Running 7 into 8 across "it's thinner each time / and soon will not hold" is the one place you *shouldn't* smooth, because that couplet's break with the preceding symmetry is the poem's payload and it needs to land as two blows, not one sentence.
- **"Spring Prospect"**: keep it. 望 is a verb of gazing-into-the-distance, so "prospect" as a noun loses that — but English hands you a free pun (prospect = view / prospect = future), and this poem is about a ruined future. Take the gift.

## What's actually good

The third couplet in version 1 — "The beacons have burned three months at a stretch; / A letter from home's worth thousands in gold" — is the best thing in the set, precisely *because* it accidentally preserves the parallel: [subject][verb][quantity] twice over. And version 2's *-old* monorhyme is the only formal analogue of the lüshi rhyme scheme anywhere here. But you pay for it in fabrication: "Grass buries the old" invents the dead, and "birdsong, overbold" inverts 鳥驚心 — the birds aren't cheeky, they jolt him.

Then version 4 wrecks the one couplet that worked: "Alarms blare; / Gold for words." is S-V against a nominal equation. The parallel is gone.

## Where I'd point the effort

Not at syllable count. At: strict grammatical parallelism in couplets 1–3; an end-stopped, ~4-beat line with a caesura at the 2+3 break; monorhyme on even lines if you can afford it without inventing content; and a final couplet that deliberately *breaks* all of the above.
