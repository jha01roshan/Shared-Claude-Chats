## Strengths of the Analysis

**1. The Central Tension is Well-Identified**

Clark effectively highlights a fundamental contradiction in Potter and Mitchell's position. They argue that:
- Agents achieve control by constraining lower-level noise and indeterminacy
- Yet somehow this same indeterminacy is supposed to enhance agency and ground moral responsibility

This is indeed a serious problem for their view. Clark's repeated emphasis on how Potter and Mitchell describe control as emerging "*in spite of*" rather than "*because of*" indeterminism effectively undermines their libertarian conclusions.

**2. The Disutility Argument is Persuasive**

Clark makes a strong case that rational agents should want their cognitive and deliberative mechanisms to be deterministic rather than indeterministic. His examples (self-driving cars, the need for reliable perception and decision-making) effectively illustrate why noise in these systems would compromise rather than enhance agency. The point that indeterminism would make computational processes less reliable in assessing options is particularly well-taken.

**3. The Distinction Between Conditional and Unconditional Ability**

Clark clearly articulates why the unconditional ability to do otherwise (granted by indeterminism) doesn't add anything meaningful to agency beyond the conditional ability already available under determinism. Since the indeterministic element isn't under the agent's control, it can't make the action more "up to" the agent.

## Areas for Potential Development

**1. Engagement with Emergent Causation**

While Clark addresses Potter and Mitchell's appeal to top-down causation, he could engage more deeply with their suggestion that indeterminacy might be necessary for macroscopic structure to emerge (as noted in footnote 4). Even if this is an empirical question, exploring the conceptual implications would strengthen the critique.

**2. The Role of Creativity and Novel Solutions**

One area where libertarians often claim indeterminism might be valuable is in creative problem-solving or generating genuinely novel responses. Clark doesn't address whether Potter and Mitchell make such claims, or how his framework would handle cases where unpredictability might seem valuable.

**3. Alternative Interpretations of "Constraint"**

Clark interprets "constraining noise" as minimizing or eliminating it. But Potter and Mitchell might argue for a more nuanced view where biological systems harness or channel indeterminacy rather than simply suppressing it. While Clark's interpretation seems more natural given their language, addressing this alternative would be helpful.

**4. The Phenomenology of Choice**

Clark briefly mentions the "widespread libertarian intuition" that we have unconditional ability to choose otherwise, but doesn't deeply engage with why this intuition is so persistent. While not necessary for his argument, addressing the phenomenological aspects might make the piece more compelling to those with libertarian intuitions.

## Minor Suggestions

1. **Technical Clarity**: The discussion of "causal dispositionalism" could be expanded slightly to make clearer why this concept doesn't rescue libertarianism from Clark's critique.

2. **Empirical Considerations**: While Clark notes that whether indeterminism exists is an empirical question, he might briefly address what empirical findings would be relevant to the philosophical issues at stake.

3. **Positive Account**: While the critique is strong, Clark could elaborate slightly more on his positive view of how moral responsibility works under determinism, beyond the brief mentions of holding each other responsible as a "practical necessity."
