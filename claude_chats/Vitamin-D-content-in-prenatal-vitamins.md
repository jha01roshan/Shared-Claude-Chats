You're right to flag it. The typical prenatal sits well below 1,000 IU.

The most-cited figure across the clinical literature and guidelines is 400 IU per tablet in most prenatal vitamins — that number traces straight back to the old IOM 200/400 IU framing and hasn't moved much despite the RDA rising to 600. The realistic range is 200 IU on the low end up to about 1,000–1,200 IU on the high end, with the bulk of products landing between 400 and 1,000 IU (10–25 mcg). So Life Extension's 1,000 IU actually sits at the *top* of the conventional range, not the bottom — most competitors give less.

A handful of the newer "premium" brands have pushed higher: Perelel and Ritual-style formulas at 2,000 IU, and a few marketing themselves specifically on vitamin D at 4,000 IU. But those are the outliers, not the average.

Your instinct about efficacy is well supported. In the study you'll recognize, women on 600 IU/day plus two glasses of fortified milk still came up 76% deficient in the mothers and 81% in the newborns. And the Hollis/Wagner MUSC RCT is the clean demonstration: 400, 2,000, and 4,000 IU arms, with 4,000 IU the dose required to achieve sufficiency and reported safe. A 1,000 IU prenatal, taken for the full nine months, simply doesn't close the gap for a woman starting deficient — which at 40–60% prevalence is most of them, and worse at your latitude.

One wrinkle worth checking if you're profiling products: the D2-versus-D3 question. Some non-prescription prenatals still use ergocalciferol (D2) rather than D3, so a labeled "1,000 IU" isn't always equivalent across brands.

The "400 IU" figure everyone cites is now a stale citation at the product level.
