Atrazine is banned in roughly 60+ countries, though it remains legal and heavily used in several major agricultural nations. Here's the picture:

**Where it's banned**

The most significant bloc is the **European Union**, which phased it out with a ban that took effect in 2004 (the decision was announced in October 2003). The EU's ban was based on a strict maximum acceptable pollutant level of 0.1 μg/L — a threshold routinely exceeded in U.S. groundwater. Notably, Germany and Italy banned atrazine even earlier, in 1991, due to its persistence in the environment, toxicity to wildlife, and human health links.

Beyond the EU, the bans extend across much of the world. The roughly 60 countries include all 27 EU members, most Middle Eastern countries, and many African countries. The Gulf Cooperation Council states have also banned it. One caveat worth noting: in many lower- and middle-income countries where atrazine is officially banned, the chemical is often smuggled in illegally.

**Where it's still used**

The United States is the notable outlier among high-income countries. Canada, Australia, Brazil, and China also continue to use atrazine to increase crop yields. In the U.S. it's the second-most widely used herbicide after glyphosate.

**Recent regulatory shifts**

Two developments post-date some of the older framing on this topic. First, the WHO's International Agency for Research on Cancer classified atrazine as "probably carcinogenic to humans" in a determination published in the January 2026 issue of The Lancet Oncology — its first assessment of the compound in nearly three decades (the prior 1998 ruling had deemed it not classifiable). Second, moving in the opposite direction, in October 2025 the U.S. EPA kept atrazine legal and weakened existing safeguards, and on May 18, 2026, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service finalized a review concluding atrazine does not pose an extinction risk to endangered species — contradicting years of the agency's own research.

Given your work on environmental exposures and "profitable ignorance" interventions, the regulatory divergence here is a fairly stark case study: the EU treated unpreventable water contamination as sufficient grounds for a ban two decades before the carcinogenicity reclassification, while the U.S. regulatory trajectory has run the other way despite a large domestic body of endocrine-disruption and contamination research. Does Atrazine require custom crops like Roundup?

## Assistant

No — and this is actually the key agronomic difference between the two chemicals, which is worth spelling out because it flips the usual GMO narrative.

Roundup (glyphosate) is *non-selective* — it kills essentially any green plant it touches. That's precisely why it requires "Roundup Ready" crops: Monsanto had to genetically engineer soybeans, corn, cotton, and canola with a bacterial gene (a glyphosate-insensitive EPSPS enzyme) so the crop survives while everything around it dies. Without the engineered trait, spraying Roundup over your field would kill the crop along with the weeds. The chemical and the transgenic seed are a matched commercial system.

Atrazine works the opposite way — it's a *selective* herbicide, and its selectivity is natural, not engineered. Corn (maize), sorghum, and sugarcane tolerate atrazine because they can metabolically detoxify it before it does damage. In corn the main route is conjugation via glutathione S-transferase, plus hydroxylation driven by benzoxazinone compounds (the DIMBOA pathway) that the plant already produces. Atrazine's mode of action is blocking photosystem II — it shuts down photosynthesis in susceptible weeds — but corn breaks the molecule down fast enough that its own photosynthesis is unaffected. This is inherent plant biochemistry, and it's why atrazine has been sprayed on corn since around 1958, decades before genetic engineering existed.

So the dependency structure is genuinely different:

Glyphosate needs a patented, engineered seed to be usable over a growing crop. Atrazine rides on a crop's own evolved detoxification chemistry and needs no special seed at all — you plant ordinary corn.

There's one partial exception worth flagging so the picture is complete: triazine-tolerant (TT) canola exists in places like Australia, but that tolerance was produced by *conventional* breeding and selection from a naturally occurring mutation, not by transgenic engineering — so it's still not the Roundup-style seed-plus-chemical package.

This distinction has an economic dimension that fits your "profitable ignorance" lens, and it cuts in an interesting direction. Glyphosate's razor-and-blades model — sell the tolerant seed, sell the matched herbicide — created a strong commercial engine to defend and expand the product. Atrazine has no such tied seed franchise; it's an old, off-patent commodity chemical made by many manufacturers. That's part of why the two face such different regulatory and lobbying dynamics, even though both are now IARC "probable carcinogens." The lack of a proprietary seed lock-in doesn't make atrazine safer, but it does change who has a financial stake in keeping it on the market and how that money is structured.
