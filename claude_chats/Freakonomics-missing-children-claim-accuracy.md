## The "7 Million Missing Children" — What Freakonomics Got Right and Wrong

### 1. The actual number of *fraudulent* dependents

Freakonomics presents the 7 million drop as essentially all fraud — the rhetorical framing ("phantom exemptions," "children who had existed only on 1040 forms") implies virtually the entire disappearance was fabricated dependents. This is significantly overstated.

The IRS's own researcher, John Szilagyi, who drafted the SSN requirement, gave a more careful estimate. His research indicated there were probably 4 to 5 million dependents being claimed illegally — either because they were fictitious or did not legally qualify as dependents. That's the *stock* of ongoing fraud at the time, not all of whom vanished in 1987.

The IRS also broke down *why* dependents disappeared in 1987:

- **~20% of the 7 million** were not fictitious at all. About 20 percent of the vanished dependents were children who had been claimed as dependents by both parents after a divorce — under the law, only one parent may claim the child as a deduction. That's roughly 1.4 million legitimate children simply being double-counted, now forced to appear only once.

- **A meaningful share** were likely *real children with no SSN yet*. It was not until 1987 that the IRS first demonstrated a program to allow parents to automatically obtain Social Security numbers for their newborn children when those births were registered, and that program did not become nationwide until 1989. Parents of real children born in the preceding years may simply not have had SSNs to provide and dropped the claim rather than deal with the paperwork.

- **A further portion** may reflect changes in the Tax Reform Act of 1986 that coincidentally hit at the same moment. Beginning in 1987, dependents were not permitted to claim a personal exemption and in most cases could claim only a limited standard deduction — this may have created some negotiation between parents and children about how long to remain a dependent. College-age children, for instance, had new incentives to *not* be claimed.

So the realistic decomposition of the 7 million is something like: **~4–5 million genuinely fraudulent**, **~1.4 million legitimate double-claims by divorced parents**, and **~0.5–1 million** real children with bureaucratic or tax-code reasons. Freakonomics presents essentially 7/7 as fraud; the best evidence points to roughly 4–5/7. That's still an extraordinary number and a great policy finding — but the rhetorical framing inflates it materially.

### 2. Other things Freakonomics got wrong or overstated

**A. The abortion-reduces-crime hypothesis — coding errors and contested evidence**

This is the book's centerpiece claim. One critique came from Christopher Foote and Christopher Goetz, two economists with the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston. They argued that Donohue and Levitt's paper contained a coding error which, when corrected, blunted their findings. Levitt acknowledged the error but argued the underlying result survived. Foote and Goetz identified: a coding mistake in the concluding regressions; that correcting the error and using a more appropriate per-capita specification generated much weaker results; and that earlier cross-state tests were not robust to allowing for differential state trends based on statewide crime rates that predate the period when abortion could have had a causal effect.

More fundamentally, independent replication has been weak. Straightforward tests using age-specific arrest and homicide rates regressed on lagged abortion rates provide little support for the Donohue-Levitt hypothesis in either the US or the United Kingdom. The Canada comparison is also damaging: Canadian violent crime followed a nearly identical rise-and-fall pattern to the US despite different abortion law timelines — something the book never addresses.

**B. The real estate agent claim — confounding variables ignored**

Freakonomics argues that agents sell their own homes for ~3% more and keep them on market ~10 days longer, implying deliberate self-dealing at clients' expense. Critics noted that Levitt and co-authors assume agents know, within better than 4%, what a house will sell for — most agents would love to have that crystal-ball knowledge, but they don't. The study also could not control for location, style, and décor features that are very important but do not appear in MLS data fields.

A simpler confound: agents who own their homes are not under the same time pressure as typical sellers. Agents selling their own homes face no risk of being fired for not selling — you're not going to fire yourself — whereas when working for others, there is a real risk of termination and the penalties associated with failure to sell (lost referrals, reputational damage). The price premium could reflect this asymmetric pressure, not information exploitation.

**C. The "missing girls" episode — crediting the wrong researcher**

Freakonomics celebrated Emily Oster's research suggesting that hepatitis B, not son preference, explained the high boy-to-girl birth ratio in Asia. Levitt praised Oster effusively when she later admitted her finding was wrong. A World Bank researcher, Monica Das Gupta, along with others in her field, had attributed the abnormally high ratio of boy-to-girl births in Asian countries to son preference manifesting in selective abortion and possibly infanticide — and Levitt never credited Das Gupta for her superior work even after Oster's error was acknowledged. The broader critique from Gelman and Fung is that Levitt suspended critical thinking about work done by friends and colleagues.

**D. The general methodological critique**

Gelman and Fung's analysis of the Freakonomics approach encountered a range of avoidable mistakes: back-of-the-envelope analyses gone wrong, unexamined assumptions, and an uncritical reliance on the work of Levitt's friends and colleagues — making it difficult for readers to discern which conclusions are fully quantitative, which are somewhat data-driven, and which are purely speculative.

This is a genuinely interesting tension in the Freakonomics chapter, because two solid studies reach what look like contradictory conclusions. Here's how to untangle them.

---

## The Freakonomics claim on black names

The book relies mainly on Fryer and Levitt's own 2004 paper, which used California birth records from 1960–2000 — over 16 million births — to track whether distinctively black names affected life outcomes. Their finding, which Freakonomics presents fairly accurately: while black names are correlated with lower income, greater likelihood of single motherhood, and a host of other worse adult outcomes, these correlations largely disappear once controlling for the childhood conditions of the individual — suggesting that "carrying a black name is primarily a consequence rather than a cause of poverty."

So far so good — that's what the data showed.

**But here's the problem:** the book then uses this finding to implicitly downplay racism as a mechanism, while separately acknowledging (almost as an afterthought) the Bertrand and Mullainathan resume study showing the opposite at the hiring stage. Those two findings don't actually contradict each other — but Freakonomics handles the tension poorly.

---

## The genuine contradiction with Bertrand & Mullainathan

Bertrand and Mullainathan sent fictitious resumes to help-wanted ads in Boston and Chicago, randomly assigning African-American- or White-sounding names to otherwise identical resumes. White names received 50% more callbacks for interviews, and callbacks were also more responsive to resume quality for white names than for black ones. The racial gap was uniform across occupation, industry, and employer size, and the study found little evidence that employers were inferring social class from the names.

That last point is important: Bertrand and Mullainathan specifically tested whether the callback gap was driven by class signaling rather than racial signaling, and concluded it wasn't. This directly challenges the Fryer-Levitt interpretation.

How do you reconcile "the name doesn't hurt your long-term outcomes" with "the name gets you 50% fewer callbacks"? The best reconciliation is that while some discriminatory employers might interview fewer minority workers, the overall effect on a black worker's life outcomes might be mitigated by other fair-minded employers. Alternatively, a distinctively black name might signal low social background, and hence potentially lower human capital — so when corrected for social background, the effect of name alone on outcomes disappears. A third possibility: ethnic names might have a direct impact on interview callbacks and unemployment duration without having a strong influence on the longer-term outcomes that Fryer and Levitt studied.

In other words, the two studies are measuring *different things* at *different points in the process* — hiring-stage discrimination vs. whole-life outcomes — and Freakonomics blurs this distinction.

---

## The deeper methodological problem: names confound race *and* class

The sharpest critique of the entire naming literature is that distinctively black names (e.g., Jamal and Lakisha) signal low socioeconomic status while typical white names do not — so the question becomes: do people not want to hire Jamal because he is Black, or because he is of low status? Even if the discrimination is real, we can't easily separate racial discrimination from class discrimination using these names. You'd need to compare black names against *low-status white names* (Bubba, Billy Bob) to isolate the racial component specifically.

On average, a Lakisha or Jamal needed 8 more years of experience to get as many callbacks as an Emily or Greg — but whether that gap is about race, class, or both remains genuinely contested.

---

## What Freakonomics actually gets wrong

Three things:

**1. It conflates two different questions.** "Does your name hurt your life outcomes?" (Fryer-Levitt: mostly no, once you control for background) is not the same as "Does your name face discrimination in hiring?" (Bertrand-Mullainathan: yes, substantially). The book presents these as roughly compatible without clearly explaining why — and the implicit message readers take away is that black names aren't much of a problem, which is too strong a conclusion.

**2. It downplays structural racism as an explanation.** The Fryer-Levitt framework was partly motivated by the question of whether *black culture* (rather than discrimination) explains Black-white economic gaps. Using the name finding to suggest culture is the main driver, while acknowledging hiring discrimination only glancingly, tilts the framing in a particular ideological direction that the data don't fully support.

**3. The long-term outcome data had weak measures.** The California records gave only broad indicators of economic achievement — not direct measures like personal income or wealth — so studying whose resumes elicit callbacks doesn't show who ultimately gets jobs or what they do once employed. Both studies have their shortcomings.

---

## The bottom line

The core Fryer-Levitt finding — that a black name is a *marker* of disadvantage rather than a *cause* — is probably correct and important. Where Freakonomics goes wrong is using it to imply that naming practices explain or reflect something about black culture that produces worse outcomes, when the hiring evidence strongly suggests that *employer discrimination* is a significant intervening mechanism. The name doesn't doom you across your whole life, but it does get you meaningfully fewer interviews — and the book doesn't sit with that tension honestly.
