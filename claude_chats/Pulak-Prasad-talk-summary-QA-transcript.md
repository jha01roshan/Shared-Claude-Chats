## The Four-Word Philosophy

Prasad opened the prepared talk with an unusual framing — a Russian experiment on Siberian foxes, where selecting only the tamest foxes across 30 generations effectively turned them into dogs. The analogy: can you find a single trait in a business that brings many other good traits along with it?

From there, he distilled Nalanda's entire philosophy into four words: **Own. Exceptional. Businesses. Forever.** He was careful about each word:

- **Own**, not "invest" — implies permanence, relationship
- **Exceptional**, not "good" or "very good"
- **Business**, not stock price or share
- **Forever**, not "long term"

From this, everything else flows in three sequential steps:

---

## Step 1: Avoid Big Risks

Prasad's central argument here is that learning *how not to invest* is actually harder and more important than learning how to invest. He frames this as the Type 1 / Type 2 error problem:

- **Type 1 error** = investing in something you shouldn't have (deer goes to drink, crocodile eats it)
- **Type 2 error** = not investing in something you should have (deer runs from a pond with no crocodile)

You cannot minimize both. Buffett's answer — "Rule #1: don't lose money. Rule #2: don't forget Rule #1" — is really saying: minimize Type 1 errors, accept Type 2. Nalanda has lost money in only **two businesses** across 19 years as the proof point.

**Categories they won't touch, ever:**

- **Poor governance** — Prasad said bluntly: "We are a nation of crooks." They outsource forensic checks on every business and walk away in most cases. Entire sectors are off the table: real estate, infrastructure, defense, mining, and anything with heavy government revenue dependence.
- **Leverage** — He's never taken a personal loan; the fund has no leverage; only 2 portfolio companies carry any debt. Reason: "If you look at the 100 top bankruptcies in the world, all of them, no exceptions, had a lot of leverage."
- **Fast-evolving industries** — Food delivery, e-commerce, microfinance, anything where the winner isn't clear. "Lot of people have made money on Zomato, but we can't figure it out, so we won't."
- **No owner in the business** — Won't invest in MNC subsidiaries (Unilever India, Nestle India), conglomerates, or PSUs. "The CEO of Nestle India doesn't have skin in the game the way Harsh Mariwala does."
- **Turnarounds** — Zero interest, regardless of how cheap. "We don't want turnarounds. We want a business that is already doing fine."

---

## Step 2: Buy High Quality at a Fair Price

**Quality = high historical ROIC.** The screening takes 20 minutes — pull a list by ROIC. The median ROIC of Nalanda's portfolio companies at time of investment: **41%.** Three businesses have delivered *infinite* returns on capital.

The "high" and "historical" parts are both non-negotiable:
- *High*: If you're earning 30-40-50% returns on capital in hyper-competitive India, something is structurally different about you.
- *Historical*: "I want at least a 5-year track record, preferably 10 years. I can't deal with stories of what you'll become."

They then spend **3–6 months** doing scuttlebutt — suppliers, customers, dealers, ex-employees, industry consultants — trying to understand *why* the business earns so much. Because the default assumption should be: "Either you're lying, or you got lucky."

Why ROIC specifically?
1. It's an objective proxy for management quality — better than any interview. "If you want to know who runs the fastest marathon, you don't interview them, you make them race."
2. It reveals capital allocation discipline over time.
3. High ROIC generates cash, which allows you to take *business risk* without *financial risk*. He used the bar analogy: if you put 10 crore into a Bangalore bar that fails, and you had borrowed that money, you're done. If you had 20 crore in the bank, you survive to fight another day.

**Fair price** = cheaper than the index on a trailing basis. Nalanda's median entry PE: **15–16x trailing**, vs the index at ~22x at the time. They never use forward earnings. They acknowledge that this creates extreme inactivity — they invested **zero dollars from February 2022 to January 2026.** The three heavy-buying moments across 19 years: six months post-Lehman, second half of 2011 (euro crisis), and March–May 2020 (pandemic). In those three months of 2020, they put in $405 million — 45% more than the previous five years combined.

## Step 3: Don't Sell

This is where Prasad parts company with most fund managers, who intellectually agree on the first two points but balk at this one.

**The compounding argument — rabbits in Australia:**
Thomas Austin imported 24 rabbits to a town called Winchelsea in Australia in 1859 and released them into the wild. 20 years later: fewer than 10,000 rabbits. 35 years later: fewer than a million. Hard to even spot them across 8 million square kilometers. But 65 years later: **10 billion rabbits.** 97% of the 10 billion came in the final 10 years. The parallel: more than 70% of Buffett's wealth came after he turned 75.

**The billionaire argument:**
Across Nalanda's portfolio of 26 businesses, there are **7 US dollar billionaires.** None of them are monitoring PE ratios and rebalancing. They're running businesses. One day they look up and realise they're a billionaire. "I would rather follow a billionaire who's made money by not doing anything to the stock price than fund managers who try to buy low and sell high."

**The Shelby Davis argument:**
In 1947, Shelby Davis — journalist, political science PhD, speechwriter, analyst — was working as deputy superintendent of the New York State insurance department. He noticed many insurance companies were trading below cash value. He borrowed $50,000 from his wife Catherine and bought them. He never sold. By 1992, his portfolio was worth half a billion dollars — with 11 of the top 12 positions being insurance companies bought in the late 40s, 50s, and early 60s, including Berkshire. He also later bought 1,500 global companies and $23 million of junk bonds that went to zero — none of it mattered, because his original insurance positions had compounded so obscenely they paid for all his sins.

**The Nalanda self-example:**
First fund: 17 investments. One loss (Mastek). Six others returned under 10% annualised. 7 out of 17 — a 40% failure rate on 780 crore. But Berger Paints, bought in 2009 for 80 crore, was worth 4,200 crore in December 2024. That one position was **5.3x the total amount invested in the seven underperformers combined.** Five other businesses individually covered the mistakes. No Nvidia — just compounding from holding quality businesses for 14–16 years.

**The only reasons Nalanda has ever sold:**
1. Redemptions (forced proportional selling, not stock selection)
2. **Bad capital allocation** — a broadcast company that wanted to start an airline; an engineering business that wanted to launch consumer finance. Full exit both times.
3. **Acquisitions** — five businesses were bought by strategic investors Nalanda didn't back. (WNS acquired by Capgemini for $3.3B — full exit.)
4. **Irreparable damage** — six times in 19 years they concluded they were wrong from the start or the business had been permanently impaired.

They never rebalance. "Either pregnant or we are not."

## Selected Q&A Points Not in the Main Talk

**On his moat being competed away:** "I got to do what I got to do. If everybody's investing in businesses I'm trying to invest in, we won't be able to. That's okay. Such is life."

**On the Graham obsolescence question:** "I don't worry about it. Because what will be will be. If I'm not able to buy anything and my commitment period expires, I'll go home. That's all right. Think about how lucky I am to be doing what I do."

**On AI:** Doesn't use it, won't invest in it. "I'd rather read the whole annual report." His team uses it marginally for historical analysis but it hasn't made much difference. On the question of whether AI makes information edge obsolete: "Information was always available. The internet has been there 30 years. AI is not information. Information is Google... I don't care, because I'm not going to change the way I think about life and investing based on some macro trend."

**On the "if you're not selling you're buying" argument:** "That's a deeply flawed way of thinking. The logical conclusion is you go to buy and sell all the time — you've basically become a day trader. It doesn't sit with me. Our philosophy is own exceptional businesses forever."

**On M&A:** Probabilistic view — not that all M&A is bad, but failure rates are extraordinary. "There's no great business in the world built on M&A, it's built organically." If it's small, infrequent, and cash-funded, it has a slightly better chance. He voted with his feet: exited a broadcast company that announced airline ambitions, and an engineering company that announced consumer finance.

**On number 2 players (re: Berger Paints):** "Acha hai, sasta hai." No different expectation from a #1. But for a #2, he wants 15–20+ years of track record proving resilience. The paints anecdote: Sherwin Williams entered India, was there 8 years, gave the business to Berger for free and walked away. Every major Indian business group including the Tatas has tried to enter paints. The industry has seen it all.

**On boards:** Won't sit on boards where promoters own 70%+ — they won't listen anyway. Open to boards where disagreement actually gets heard.

**On retail investors:** "Please do not invest in individual stocks. 80–90% of fund managers who do this from morning to evening can't beat the market. Just do SIP in the index or select funds and forget about it."

**On his temperament:** "I realised when I was 34 that I'm a little bit of a psychopath — I don't get sad when prices fall, don't feel happy when they rise. In any other career this would have been useless. In investing, the ability to sleep is a great blessing."

**On his VC friend at lunch:** Had lunch with a VC friend whose two portfolio companies had IPO'd that week. The friend couldn't stop checking his phone, eyes on the price the whole time. "What's it worth, living such a kind of life, where you cannot enjoy a lunch, your kids don't know you."

**On philosophy without execution:** "Can't claim a philosophy and not buy during the pandemic. Can't get scared and wait two weeks for the right price. Can't talk governance and be 50% invested in Adani. The philosophy only works if you actually do it."

**On the great companies lasting longer than people think:** "People consistently underestimate the power and duration of survival of companies." Last year's Economist found that **56% of the Fortune 500 — 280 companies — were created before World War II.** The average age of Fortune 500 companies in 1990 was 75 years; it's now 90. MRF wasn't #1 in tyres in 1993 when he was consulting; it is now.

**On why he left private equity:** At Warburg, they owned 10% of HDFC mortgage finance. Put in $70M, it became $210M in two years. Exited at $210M. "Simply the wrong decision. I was frustrated with the PE business model that didn't allow me to hold great businesses for the very long run."

**On his day:** Reading and talking to portfolio companies. That's it.

**On noise cancellation:** No social media (not even LinkedIn). No TV at home. No price-tracking apps. Doesn't meet fund managers. "No dessert in the fridge" — if temptation isn't there, you don't succumb to it.

## The Writer's Own Learnings

The note-taker ended with four observations of their own:

1. **He plays to his temperament and reduces Type 1 errors** — his edge is behavioural, not analytical. He's structured the fund to express that edge cleanly.
2. **It's structurally hard to replicate** — committed capital that he calls at will, not AUM sitting as cash that must be deployed. Saying no to deployment is very hard without that structure.
3. **It's an emotional game far more than a numbers one** — the writer admitted they were looking at a cheap low-quality turnaround the very next morning after attending this talk.
4. **It doesn't have to be this complex** — cut macro, cut geopolitical noise, focus on quality, hold for the very long term. Basic and known. But the investable universe expands dramatically once you start thinking beyond 2–5 years — which is probably why the room felt calm.

## 3–5 Big Takeaways

**1. The committed capital structure is the strategy, not a feature of the strategy.**
Prasad didn't invest a single dollar for almost four years (Feb 2022–Jan 2026). That's only possible if you're not sitting on deployed AUM that has fee pressure or LP anxiety attached to it. His PE background gave him the insight to raise public market money on a PE commitment structure — and he credits timing and luck for getting away with it in the frothy 2007 environment. Most institutional investors have cash that needs to go to work. For a VC, the analogy is stark: the discipline to *not* deploy from a fund when quality isn't available at fair price is the core of the edge — and it requires LP relationships that can tolerate that. The structural implication for early-stage VC is real: vintage concentration (deploying most of a fund in one or two windows) is probably underappreciated as a value driver relative to steady-state deployment.

**2. Temperament is the actual moat — and it's non-transferable.**
Prasad is explicit that his psychopathic equanimity around price movements is a *gift* he's exploiting, not a skill he built. He can't teach it; he can only select for it in his team over time. Two of his four investing colleagues are "very much in tune"; two have learned through experience. The implication for founders and VCs is the same: your actual edge is usually a dispositional fact about you, not a framework you've adopted. The frameworks Prasad uses (ROIC screen, scuttlebutt, trailing PE vs index) are not secret or complex — anyone can read them. What most can't replicate is acting on them when everything is falling apart. The lesson is to know your own temperament honestly and structure your life and portfolio around it, rather than aspiring to someone else's.

**3. For early-stage VC in India: Prasad's "poor nation, lots of room" thesis is a strong tail-wind argument for your asset class specifically.**
Prasad's India thesis is simple: we're 1/5th the size of China by consumption, we're poor, we have miles to catch up, so growth will persist in traditional businesses without needing to reach for unproven new categories. He doesn't need to bet on Zomato or new-age names. For early-stage VC, the inverse of this is actually more interesting: the categories that *are* growing faster than "economy + inflation" — the ones Prasad won't touch because the winner isn't clear yet — are exactly where early-stage bets make sense. His framework for what he *won't* touch (fast-evolving industries, no track record, uncertain competitive structure) is essentially a map of where early-stage VC must play. The insight for you: Prasad's conservatism implicitly validates the opportunity set you're investing into. The businesses that eventually become "exceptional, owner-run, high-ROIC compounders" that he'll buy 10–15 years from now are the ones you're trying to back at the seed stage today.

**4. The "don't sell" principle has a direct VC analogy — and most funds violate it systematically.**
Prasad's critique of PE — that the fundraising cycle forces you to sell great businesses in 3–5 years just to show DPI and raise the next fund — is structurally identical to the pressure early-stage VCs face to return capital from early winners. The rabbits / Buffett wealth curve point is directly applicable: the biggest returns are always back-loaded, and selling a Berger Paints at 2x in year 4 to show IRR is almost always the wrong decision in hindsight. Prasad's Nalanda Fund 1 example — where Berger at 80 crore became 4,200 crore and covered five other failures — should make any early-stage VC think hard about secondary sale decisions, pro-rata follow-on discipline, and the structural temptation to harvest winners early. The best argument for patient capital at the fund level isn't philosophical, it's arithmetical.

**5. The forensic governance filter is the most underrated part of the framework — and arguably most relevant to early-stage India.**
Prasad estimates that 70–80% of listed companies in his universe get rejected on governance alone before any quality or valuation analysis begins. He uses external forensic agencies and avoids entire sectors structurally (government-revenue-heavy businesses especially). At early stage, you obviously can't run forensic checks on seed-stage founders, but the principle maps: most of the variance in Indian startup outcomes is founder integrity and alignment, not market size or product quality. The explicit, systematic elimination of the bottom of the governance distribution — before getting excited about business quality — is probably the correct sequencing. In Indian early-stage VC, governance issues that surface at Series B or C almost always had precursor signals much earlier. Building a version of the forensic filter into the diligence process, rather than treating governance as a residual question after conviction, is the takeaway.
