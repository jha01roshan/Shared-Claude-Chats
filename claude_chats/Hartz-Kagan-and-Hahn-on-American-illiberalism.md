These are three competing accounts of the same phenomenon — American illiberalism/authoritarianism — each staking out a different label, all published or centered in 2024:

**Louis Hartz** (*The Liberal Tradition in America*, 1955) is the baseline "liberal" thesis: because the U.S. never had a real feudal past, it developed a single dominant liberal (Lockean) consensus rather than Europe's sharp left/right extremes — the country lacked the extremes of left and right found in Western Europe because it wasn't born out of a feudal tradition, with debate perpetually taking place within rigid ideological limits.

**Steven Hahn** (*Illiberal America: A History*, 2024) argues this is wrong — illiberalism (racism, nativism, authoritarianism) isn't a deviation from an otherwise liberal tradition, it's coequal and baked in from the start: illiberalism was never fringe as Hartz's generation believed; rather it inspired law and elected officials, built political movements, and spawned mob action. He explicitly writes to dismantle Hartz's framework.

**Robert Kagan** (*Rebellion: How Antiliberalism Is Tearing America Apart—Again*, 2024) picks a different word on purpose. He distinguishes "illiberal" from "anti-liberal": illiberalism sounds like misbehaving without necessarily realizing it or being hostile to liberalism, whereas anti-liberalism signals real ideological opposition to liberalism itself. So Kagan's frame is about active, willful hostility to liberal order, not just falling short of it.

Worth noting: one review argues Kagan and Hahn, despite the different labels, end up doing similar work — each locates Trumpism at the end of a long counter-liberal tradition, and the reviewer found that move somewhat exhausted of fresh political insight.

The reviewer is Sean Byrnes, writing in *Dissent* (so a critique from the left, notably). His "exhausted" charge has a specific shape. He argues that both books are entries in the "what's the matter with Kansas" genre — inquiries organized around the question "What is wrong with them?" — and that this question has stopped producing anything. His evidence for exhaustion: Hahn's readers will likely draw much the same conclusions as Kagan's readers — there have been lots of terrible people in history, and Trump and his voters are among them — which lands you right back at Scarborough and Krugman asking how anyone could be that stupid. The genealogy explains nothing because it terminates in bafflement.

And you're right that he's making a just-so-story charge, most sharply against Kagan: Rebellion flattens the past into a narrative about some bad people who happen to share certain characteristics with Trump, but doesn't tell us much about why they believe what they do, or how they might be stopped. A genealogy that only establishes resemblance is post-hoc pattern-matching — the demon has always been with us, and here it is again.

Byrnes's own answer to "what would fresh insight look like" is materialist, as you'd expect from *Dissent*: flip the question to "What might be wrong with us?" and look at the forty-year marriage of expanding social equality to grinding economic inequality. His most interesting analytical move is buried in his reading of Hahn: some have experienced the advance of liberalism as a program of class warfare rather than one of liberation. That's a real insight — illiberalism as a rational (if misdirected) response to something liberalism actually did, not a demon or a disposition.

But your question points at a *different* kind of fresh insight, and I think it's actually the more neglected one. Byrnes takes discontent seriously as material grievance. Nobody in this exchange takes the postliberals seriously as *thinkers* — as people making an argument that could be evaluated on its merits rather than diagnosed.

What would that look like? Taking Deneen, Vermeule, Ahmari, Pappin at their word means treating claims like these as claims, not symptoms:

That liberalism fails by succeeding — its logic of liberation, applied consistently, dissolves the pre-liberal inheritances (family, church, guild, neighborhood, moral consensus) that liberal societies quietly depended on. Deneen's *Why Liberalism Failed* thesis. Note this is structurally almost the *same* observation as Byrnes's — liberalism as solvent experienced as warfare by those whose worlds it dissolved — except the postliberals say the dissolved thing was culture and cult, not just wages.

That liberal "neutrality" is a fiction; every regime enforces a substantive vision of the good, and liberalism's pretense of neutrality just means its vision wins without having to argue for itself. So the postliberal project isn't "impose values where there were none" but "contest the values already being imposed." Right or wrong, that's an argument with a long pedigree (it's basically MacIntyre's, whom you know well — and MacIntyre pointedly refused the political conclusions his postliberal admirers drew).

That the common good is a real object of politics, not an aggregation of preferences — Vermeule's constitutional version of this being the most legally consequential.

Here's what taking them at their word buys you analytically. First, it explains the Trump alliance *better* than the demon theory does: it becomes legible as a strategic wager by people who believe the liberal order is already lost, not an expression of shared id. Which also predicts the alliance's instability — Trump is a liberationist of appetite, about as far from Aquinas as one gets, and the postliberals know it. A genealogy of illiberal darkness can't see that fault line; an intellectual-history-taken-seriously can. Second, it lets you locate where they're actually wrong, which is more useful than declaring them descended from Tillman. My candidate: the diagnosis (liberalism has unacknowledged substantive commitments and eats its own preconditions) is considerably stronger than the prescription (therefore a state powerful enough to enforce the good), and the prescription fails on the postliberals' own Augustinian premises — the same fallen creatures who corrupted the liberal order will staff the postliberal one. Burke would ask where the inherited institutions for their project are; the honest answer is that they'd have to be built by exactly the centralized rationalism they claim to oppose.

There's a nice symmetry worth noting for your purposes: Byrnes, Hahn, and the postliberals all agree against Kagan and Hartz that the "liberal tradition" narrative is a mid-century construction that conceals as much as it reveals. They just disagree about what it conceals — class domination, illiberal coexistence, or theological substance. That triangle might be a more generative frame than any of the three corners alone.
