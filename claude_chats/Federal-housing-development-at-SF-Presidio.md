Short version: illegal under current law, legal if Congress wants it to be, and physically/politically wild at that scale. Let me break it down.

**Legality under current law: no.** The Presidio sits inside a nested set of statutory constraints. It's part of the Golden Gate National Recreation Area, and the Presidio Trust Act of 1996 requires the Trust to manage the interior ~80% of the park consistent with the purposes of the GGNRA Act and the 1994 General Management Plan Amendment — i.e., park, recreation, and preservation purposes. On top of that, the entire Presidio is a National Historic Landmark District, and the Trust Act specifically directs management in a manner protective of that status. The Presidio Trust Management Plan (2002) caps total built square footage in the park at roughly what existed when the Army left (~5.6M sq ft), so even the Trust's own governing plan forbids net densification at scale. A President can't do this by executive order, HUD can't do it, and the Trust itself can't do it — the Trust's authority to demolish and build is bounded by the statute and the NHL designation. Any attempt would also trigger NEPA and NHPA Section 106 review, and demolishing large swaths of an NHL district for towers would be about the most litigable federal action imaginable.

**Legality with new legislation: yes, cleanly.** Congress has plenary power over federal property under the Property Clause (Art. IV, §3). It created the GGNRA and the Trust by statute and can amend or repeal both by statute. A hypothetical "Presidio Housing Act" that carved land out of the GGNRA, dissolved or repurposed the Trust, exempted the project from the PTMP, and (if Congress wanted) overrode NEPA/NHPA review would be constitutional. There's no state or local veto — CEQA doesn't apply to federal land, and SF zoning has no jurisdiction over the Presidio. Congress could even statutorily deem environmental review complete, as it's done for other projects. So the legal barrier is entirely political: you need a majority of both houses plus a presidential signature to bulldoze one of the most beloved national park sites in the country.

**Physical feasibility: the math is brutal.** The Presidio is ~1,490 acres, but a big chunk is coastal bluffs, forest, Crissy Field, the national cemetery, and the golf course. Call developable land generously 400–600 acres if you're willing to raze most of the historic districts and much of the forest. 100,000 units on 500 acres is 200 units/acre net — that's Hong Kong/Manhattan tower density across the whole footprint, plus ~200,000+ residents in a park currently housing about 3,000 people across roughly 1,400 units. Infrastructure is the real killer: the Presidio has no rail transit, one freeway touching it (101/Doyle Drive feeding the Golden Gate Bridge, which is already at capacity), and utilities sized for a decommissioned Army post — the Trust is currently doing water, sewer, storm drainage, and electrical upgrades just to support one 196-unit project. You'd essentially be building a new city of Fremont's population on a peninsula tip served by Muni buses and a shuttle.

**Calibration on what's actually happening:** the live version of "housing on Presidio federal land" is the Letterman Residential Project — 196 studio, one- and two-bedroom apartments on the old Letterman Hospital site, which cleared its final Environmental Assessment and historic preservation review in May 2026, with construction targeted for mid-2027 and occupancy by early 2029. That's the revealed pace of Presidio housing production: ~200 units, on already-disturbed non-historic land, replacing existing buildings, after multi-year review — and it's genuinely the biggest residential addition since the Army left. 100,000 units is 500x that.

If the actual policy question is "where could the feds meaningfully add housing supply in SF," the more defensible version is a much smaller number — a few thousand units on the Presidio's non-historic, already-developed sites (Letterman, the Public Health Service district, surface parking, the Wherry-style housing areas) — which would still require amending the PTMP's square footage cap and probably the Trust Act, but wouldn't require destroying the park. The 100,000 figure works as a provocation about federal land power; as a proposal it dies on transit capacity alone before you even reach the politics of demolishing a National Historic Landmark.

## Assistant

Fun problem. The design constraint that makes this "defensible" rather than fantasy: touch almost nothing people actually love about the Presidio. Crissy Field, the coastal bluffs and batteries, the Main Post parade ground, the cemetery, and the forest stay untouched. You can get to 15,000 units using maybe 250–300 acres — under 20% of the park — and the park arguably comes out *better*, because most of that acreage is currently golf course, parking lots, decommissioned 1950s Army housing, and dead zones. Here's the plan.

## The Presidio Homes Act (the legal chassis)

Everything hangs on one statute, because none of this is legal today. The Act would:

- Amend the Presidio Trust Act to add housing production as a co-equal statutory purpose and lift the PTMP's ~5.6M sq ft cap for designated development zones.
- Statutorily deem NEPA/NHPA review complete for the zoned areas (precedent: Congress deemed all approvals granted for the Mountain Valley Pipeline in 2023), with a jurisdiction-stripping provision routing any challenge directly to the D.C. Circuit on a 90-day clock.
- Appropriate the money directly — call it $18–20B over 12 years — so nothing depends on tax credit syndication or SF's approval processes.
- Keep the Trust as master developer (it has 30 years of delivery credibility and just cleared the 196-unit Letterman project), but give it eminent-domain-free, zoning-free authority within the zones.

SF gets no veto — federal land — but you buy the city in with a payment-in-lieu-of-taxes deal and a seat on a design review board with advisory power only.

## The five development zones (~15,000 units, ~50,000 residents)

**1. Letterman East Village + Trump Tower West — ~4,200 units.** The 60-acre Letterman District at the Lombard Gate is the most urban corner of the park, already has an approved 196-unit project and utility upgrades underway, and abuts the Marina's street grid. Build it out at 5–8 stories, Barcelona-style perimeter blocks. The marquee: **Trump Tower West** at the gate itself — a ~40-story, ~450-foot hotel/condo/observation-deck tower, the tallest building in northern San Francisco, with Golden Gate Bridge views from the top floors and a free public observation level (the political sweetener). Siting it at Lombard Gate matters: it reads as an extension of the city skyline rather than an object in the park, and it's the one location where a tower doesn't photobomb the bridge from Crissy Field. Naming a federal building requires legislation anyway — it goes in the Act, and it's transparently the price of presidential championship. Fine; say so out loud.

**2. The Golf Course Neighborhood — ~6,500 units.** This is the big lever and the big fight. The Presidio Golf Course is ~150 acres of publicly-owned land reserved for roughly 200 golfers a day, sitting directly adjacent to Presidio Heights and the Richmond. Take ~110 acres for a mid-rise family neighborhood — 4–7 stories, 60 units/acre net, courtyard buildings, 45%+ two- and three-bedroom units — organized around a new 25-acre central park and two SFUSD elementary schools plus a middle school (SFUSD is closing schools for lack of children; 50,000 new residents is the enrollment rescue plan, which is a genuinely potent political frame). Keep 9 holes as a compact course plus a First Tee youth facility. "Homes for 20,000 families vs. 18 holes" is the most winnable land-use argument in America.

**3. Wherry/Baker Beach redevelopment — ~2,400 units.** The southwestern housing areas are non-historic 1950s–60s Army stock, currently ~500 low-slung units. Replace at 5x density with terraced mid-rise stepping down toward the coast, well back from the bluffs.

**4. Public Health Service District expansion — ~1,100 units.** The 15th Avenue Gate area is already a converted residential pocket bordering the Richmond. Infill its surface lots and edges; it plugs straight into the Geary and California corridors.

**5. Fort Scott adaptive reuse + Main Post infill — ~800 units.** Fort Scott's barracks are half-empty and beautiful; convert to housing rather than the conference-center concepts that keep dying. Add liner buildings on Main Post parking lots. This zone is also your speed play — see timeline.

Program-wide: 30% permanently affordable (federal ownership makes this trivially cheap — no LIHTC gymnastics), preferences for teachers, nurses, and first responders, and Trust-owned rentals alongside 99-year ground-lease condos so the government recoups money over time.

## Transit

50,000 residents on a peninsula corner with no rail is the real design problem. Three layers:

**BRT (years 1–4, ~$450M).** Two lines. **Lombard BRT** extending the existing Van Ness BRT west along Lombard/Richardson into Letterman and across Doyle Drive — this is the workhorse and it's mostly paint, signal priority, and stations on an existing corridor. **Presidio–Geary BRT** running California/Presidio Ave to link the Golf Course Neighborhood and PHSD to the Geary corridor and future Geary improvements. Upgrade PresidiGo into a free, frequent internal circulator connecting all five zones.

**Crissy Field ferry (years 2–3, ~$120M).** A terminal near the East Beach connecting to the Ferry Building and Larkspur. Cheap, fast to build, and it's the single most scenic commute in the United States — this is your ribbon-cutting before any tunnel opens.

**The tunnel: Central Subway Phase 3 (years 3–12, ~$6–8B).** Extend the T-Third from its Chinatown tail tracks — which were literally built pointing north for this purpose — under North Beach (station boxes partially exist from TBM extraction), Fisherman's Wharf, the Marina (Chestnut station), terminating at Letterman/Trump Tower West. Four miles, four stations, one seat from the Presidio to Union Square, Moscone, and Caltrain. Federal money and a statutory streamlining title (design-build, single EIS deemed complete, FTA delivery reforms) are the only reasons to believe a better cost/schedule than SF's track record; target 9 years, promise 12. I'd skip a second tunnel — a Geary subway is the better citywide project but it's a separate $10B fight; the BRT link to Geary keeps that option open.

## Timeline to first move-ins

Assume the Act passes January 2027.

- **2027:** Trust stands up delivery org; design-build procurements; Letterman utilities work (already underway) expands.
- **2028:** Fort Scott adaptive reuse construction begins (existing buildings = fastest path); expanded Letterman phase 1 breaks ground on the already-cleared EA footprint; Lombard BRT construction; ferry terminal begins.
- **2029: first residents.** Fort Scott conversions (~300 units) plus the original 196 Letterman units open — that's roughly 30 months from enactment, which tracks the Trust's own current schedule.
- **2030–31:** Letterman East Village opens in waves (~1,500 units); ferry and both BRT lines running; golf course infrastructure and grading complete, vertical construction starts; Trump Tower West tops out (towers are slow — call it a 2032 opening).
- **2032–35:** The big wave — Golf Course Neighborhood and Wherry deliver ~2,000–2,500 units/year at peak, which is aggressive but achievable with parallel design-build packages and modular construction on cleared, single-owner, unzoned land. Cumulative ~9,000 units by end of 2034.
- **2035–38:** Buildout to 15,000; subway opens ~2036–38.

So: **first keys in ~2.5 years, half the program housed by year 8, complete by year 11–12.** For comparison, that's roughly Treasure Island's entire multi-decade entitlement-plus-construction arc compressed by two-thirds — the compression comes entirely from Congress deleting the approval process and writing checks instead of financing deals.

The honest weak points if you're stress-testing this: the golf course fight will be vicious and well-funded (Presidio Heights neighbors are not a constituency that loses often); the NHPA override will generate a preservation-community firestorm even though the historic districts are barely touched; and the tower-in-a-national-park-site precedent is the thing opponents will nationalize. The counter-story you'd run: 80%+ of the park untouched or improved, a new 25-acre park, schools refilled, nurses and teachers living in a national park, and the federal government building homes directly for the first time in two generations.
