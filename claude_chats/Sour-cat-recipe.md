## Assistant

Yeah, absolutely — roosters are standard livestock and home butchery is a normal skill. Quick heads up before the how-to: an old rooster is *tough*. Like, genuinely tough. The muscle is dense and full of connective tissue, so the cooking method matters as much as the butchering. Don't try to roast it like a supermarket chicken — you'll get shoe leather.

Here's the full path:

**Butchering**

You'll want a sharp knife, a killing cone (or a sturdy way to restrain the bird), a pot of water heated to about 65°C / 150°F for scalding, and a clean work surface. Withhold food for 12–24 hours beforehand so the crop and intestines are emptier — makes evisceration much cleaner.

The kill itself: invert the bird in the cone (this calms them and positions the head), then sever the jugular and carotid on one side of the neck with a quick deep cut. Let it bleed out fully — 2–3 minutes. Don't rush this; residual blood ruins the meat.

Scald by dunking the whole bird in the 65°C water for about 60 seconds, swishing it around. Test by tugging a wing feather — if it slides out easily, you're done. Pluck immediately while warm; it gets harder as the skin cools.

Evisceration: cut around the vent carefully (don't pierce the intestine), then make a small horizontal opening above it and reach in to pull out the entire cavity contents in one mass. Save the heart, liver (cut the green gallbladder off cleanly — if it bursts on the liver, that part is bitter and ruined), and gizzard (split it, peel out the inner lining and grit). Remove the head, feet (scald separately and peel the yellow skin if you want them for stock), and the oil gland at the base of the tail.

Rest the carcass in the fridge, uncovered or loosely covered, for **at least 24–48 hours** before cooking. This is non-negotiable for an older bird — rigor mortis needs to pass and enzymes need to start tenderizing the meat. Skipping this gives you rubber.

**Cooking**

Now the important part. For a mature rooster, your three best options are:

*Coq au vin* is the classic answer and exists specifically because of this problem. Joint the bird, marinate overnight in red wine with aromatics, then brown and braise low (around 150°C / 300°F) for 2–3 hours until fork-tender. The wine's acidity and the long braise break down the connective tissue into gelatin.

*Stock + confit split* — break it down into legs/thighs (confit them in their own fat or duck fat at 90°C for 4 hours, then store submerged in fat) and use the carcass, wings, and back for an absurdly rich stock. Roosters make better stock than any chicken you'll buy.

*Pressure cooker / Instant Pot* if you want it done in one evening — 45 minutes at high pressure with stock, aromatics, and wine, then reduce the liquid into a sauce. Less romantic, works great.

What you want to avoid: roasting whole, grilling, pan-frying breasts. Anything fast and dry will fight you.

What's the bird's age, roughly? Under a year you have more options; over that and braising is really the move.
