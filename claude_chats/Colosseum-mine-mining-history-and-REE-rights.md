## Colosseum Mine: Minerals Mined Beyond Gold

Yes — the Colosseum Mine has always been a multi-mineral deposit, not purely a gold operation.

**Silver** was co-produced alongside gold. Gold occurs partly as electrum (a natural gold-silver alloy), with the remainder present in silver minerals. Other metals found in the ore include lead, zinc, copper, arsenic, and mercury, though very little mercury was actually found.

**Silver** was formally listed as a secondary commodity alongside gold, copper, and lead in federal mining records. Official commodity records list gold as the primary product with copper, lead, and silver as secondary commodities.

**The broader Clark Mountain District** where Colosseum sits has always been minerally diverse. The district includes the Mountain Pass rare earth mine seven miles south, numerous abandoned copper mines, and scattered fluorite, antimony, and tungsten prospects.

So while gold dominated commercial production, silver was consistently co-extracted, and the ore body contains a range of associated base metals.

## REE Mining Rights: Additional Requirements?

This is where it gets very interesting — and quite legally contentious right now.

**The current position of Dateline Resources and the BLM (Trump administration)** is that no separate REE mining rights are required. The U.S. Department of the Interior publicly confirmed that "Dateline holds valid existing rights under the Mining Act and the California Desert Protection Act, including the right to explore for and extract rare earth elements in addition to gold." Their argument is that the existing Plan of Operations (originally approved by BLM in the 1980s) covers REE extraction under the same rights.

**However, there is significant dispute about this.** Experts say the Trump administration is ignoring standard procedures. "What's concerning to me about the Colosseum Mine is that it doesn't seem to be following a regulatory process that would provide an opportunity or requirement to even go out and do preconstruction surveys," said Jim Andre, director of UC Riverside's Granite Mountains Desert Research Center.

**The National Park Service disagrees** with the BLM's position. The Park Service's position, as outlined in a June 2023 letter, maintained that the 1995 decision approving operations was only a temporary approval for continued reclamation work and tailings pond water treatment and monitoring — not a blanket approval for new mining.

**Why REE mining normally requires additional steps:**

Under standard federal mining law on BLM-managed land, expanding the scope of mining to new commodities (especially critical minerals) would typically require:
- An updated or amended Plan of Operations
- A new or supplemented Environmental Impact Statement (EIS)
- Section 106 cultural resource review
- Consultation under the Endangered Species Act (especially relevant here given desert tortoise habitat)
- Coordination with the managing federal agency (in this case, the National Park Service, since the land became the Mojave National Preserve in 1994)

**What's actually happening:** The federal government has made no secret that it hopes Dateline will extract rare earths, citing national security. "For too long, the United States has depended on foreign adversaries like China for rare earth elements for technologies that are vital to our national security," the BLM stated — an announcement that came just days after China banned rare earth exports in retaliation for U.S. tariffs.

Sen. Adam Schiff and three Democratic colleagues have objected to the BLM's approval and are calling for a thorough environmental impact assessment and a legal rationale for the decision to permit REE activity.

**Bottom line:** Under normal procedures, adding REE extraction to an existing gold mining right would require amended permits, updated environmental review, and agency coordination. In Colosseum's case, the Trump administration has effectively short-circuited that process by declaring the existing rights sufficient — a position that is legally contested and politically controversial.
