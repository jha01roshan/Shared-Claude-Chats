**The story itself: not a study.** It comes from *Art & Fear* (Bayles & Orland, 1993), where it's told as a ceramics class. Ted Orland told James Clear by email in 2016 that the real origin was a gambit photographer Jerry Uelsmann used to motivate his beginning photography students at the University of Florida, and that he swapped ceramics for photography in the retelling because he and Bayles wanted to broaden the range of media referenced in the book. So: one instructor's recollection of one class, no measurements, no control, retold secondhand with acknowledged literary license. The absence of details like the teacher's name is what made it read as apocryphal to people for years.

**The underlying claim: yes, tested, and it partly holds up.**

- Paulus and colleagues ran a brainstorming experiment with four instruction sets — no specific focus, quantity goal, quality goal, or both. The quantity-goal condition beat the other three on both number of ideas and number of *good* ideas, supporting Osborn's 1953 assumption that a quantity focus is best for brainstorming. Notably, the combined quantity+quality goal didn't help.
- Incentive experiments point the same way. In one, participants paid per idea regardless of quality initially did worse than controls, but on returning to the task ten days later showed a clear creativity advantage over the other groups on both quality and quantity — the effect needed an incubation gap to show up.
- The broader theoretical frame is Simonton's "equal-odds rule": the ratio of hits to total works is roughly constant within a creator, so total output predicts number of successes. That's been tested behaviorally and even with neuroimaging (e.g. a poetry-composition study examining generation versus revision phases).

**Caveats.** The evidence base is mostly short brainstorming sessions with undergraduates, not semester-long skill acquisition with a physical craft. And it isn't uniform: specific, difficult *novelty* goals have been shown to improve idea novelty, suggesting well-specified quality goals aren't inherently harmful — the problem may be vague quality goals. And one economics experiment found incentives moved quantity and average quality but not average originality.

So the honest version: the photography/ceramics anecdote is a parable, not data, but the mechanism it dramatizes — that a vague "make it perfect" goal induces paralysis while a volume goal produces reps and feedback — has independent experimental support in the ideation literature.
