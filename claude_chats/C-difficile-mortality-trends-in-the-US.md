A few different numbers circulate depending on what's being counted, so the honest answer has to distinguish them.

**Current annual deaths**

- About 29,000 patients died within 30 days of an initial C. difficile diagnosis, of which roughly 15,000 were estimated to be directly attributable to the infection (CDC, 2011 surveillance year, published 2015).
- A later CDC revision using improved methods put it lower: about 12,800 deaths per year out of an estimated 223,900 cases (2017 data, published 2019). The drop reflects both genuine decline in healthcare-associated CDI and tighter case definitions, not just fewer deaths.
- Review literature typically cites the rough range of 15,000–30,000 deaths annually.

So "the" number is somewhere between ~13,000 (directly attributable, recent) and ~29,000 (any death within 30 days of diagnosis). The spread is mostly a definitional artifact.

**The trend over time**

This is where the increase is dramatic. Using death-certificate data (C. difficile listed as underlying or contributing cause):

- Mortality rose from 5.7 deaths per million in 1999 to 23.7 per million in 2004 — roughly a 35% increase per year over that window.
- A 1999–2023 analysis found age-adjusted mortality climbing from 0.72 to 2.17 per 100,000, peaking at 4.78 — i.e., a ~3x net rise with a mid-period peak around 6–7x baseline, then partial decline.

**The 40-years-ago comparison**

Going back to ~1986 is harder because there's no clean national CDI mortality series that far back, and ICD coding changes complicate any direct comparison. But the qualitative picture is firm: C. difficile was only identified as the cause of pseudomembranous/antibiotic-associated colitis in 1978, and through the 1980s it was an uncommon, generally mild, treatable condition with low attributable mortality. The explosion came around 2000 with the emergence of the hypervirulent NAP1/BI/027 strain (the same one flagged in the literature above), which drove both incidence and case-fatality sharply upward. So the increase from the mid-1980s isn't 2–3x — it's more like one to two orders of magnitude, going from a clinical footnote to the deadliest of the tracked superbugs in the US.

One caveat on the recent numbers: some of the apparent burden inflation is a testing artifact — PCR-based testing can over-report by detecting colonization rather than active disease, which matters if you're comparing eras with different diagnostic methods.

This is a sensitive topic for anyone affected by a C. diff death — happy to pull the full CDC WONDER series or the age-stratified breakdown (80%+ of deaths are in the 65+ group) if that's useful for a page.
