Short answer: yes, it's First Amendment territory, but the constitutional problem is narrower than people assume, and the legislative path is more about *which legal hook you use* than about repealing the First Amendment. Here's the landscape.

**What exists today (all disclosure/friction, no ban):**

- **FARA (1938)** — CGTN America, Xinhua, China Daily, etc. have been required to register and label their affiliation with Beijing, disclosing budget and ownership structure. The Supreme Court blessed this approach in *Meese v. Keene* (1987): the government can force a "political propaganda" label, it just can't suppress the content.
- **Communications Act § 310** — foreign governments can't hold US broadcast licenses. This is why CGTN has never had an over-the-air license; it rides on cable carriage agreements, satellite, YouTube, and apps, none of which are FCC-licensed the way broadcast is.
- **Foreign Missions Act designations (2020)** — State designated CCTV, Xinhua, CGTN, China Radio International, and the China Daily distributor as foreign missions, requiring personnel lists and real estate disclosures and capping headcount, but without ordering them out or limiting their activities.
- **Soft chokepoints** — CGTN lost congressional press gallery credentials because gallery rules bar registered foreign agents. RT America died in 2022 not via government ban but because DirecTV and Roku dropped it — private carriage decisions are fully legal and need no legislation at all.

**What would have to change to actually ban it:**

1. **Amend or repeal the Berman Amendment.** This is the most concrete statutory answer to your question. IEEPA — the workhorse for sanctioning foreign adversaries — contains an explicit carve-out (added 1988, expanded 1994) exempting "information and informational materials" from sanctions precisely so the president *can't* embargo foreign publications, films, and broadcasts. Treasury cannot OFAC-sanction CCTV's content distribution as long as that exemption stands. Congress wrote that carve-out deliberately after Cold War-era seizures of Cuban and Soviet materials; undoing it would be the cleanest path to an executive-branch ban, and it's been floated repeatedly in TikTok-adjacent debates.

2. **A PAFACA-style statute.** The TikTok divest-or-ban law is the modern template: don't ban the *speech*, ban US entities (app stores, cable operators, CDNs, ad networks) from *distributing or transacting with* a foreign-adversary-controlled outlet. Congress could pass "PAFACA for state media" tomorrow. The catch is what the Supreme Court actually held in *TikTok v. Garland* (Jan 2025): it upheld the law on **data security** grounds and conspicuously declined to rest on the government's content-manipulation/propaganda rationale. CCTV doesn't harvest American user data the way TikTok does, so the winning argument from that case mostly doesn't transfer. A statute whose stated purpose is "Americans shouldn't see CCP propaganda" is a content- and viewpoint-based restriction that draws strict scrutiny.

3. **Extend § 310-style foreign-government ownership bars to cable/streaming carriage.** The UK shows the licensing model works — Ofcom revoked CGTN's license in 2021 because the license was wrongfully held and UK law requires licensees to have genuine editorial control. But the US never built a licensing regime for cable channels or internet video, and constructing one now would collide with *Turner Broadcasting* and *Reno v. ACLU* — cable and the internet get much stronger First Amendment protection than spectrum-scarce broadcast does.

**The actual First Amendment problem:** it's not CCTV's rights — foreign governments and foreign corporations operating abroad have essentially no First Amendment claims (*AID v. Alliance for Open Society II*, 2020). The rights at stake belong to (a) **American audiences**, per *Lamont v. Postmaster General* (1965), which struck down a scheme requiring Americans to affirmatively request "communist political propaganda" mail and established a right to *receive* foreign speech, and (b) **American distributors and platforms**, whose decision to carry a channel is itself protected editorial activity. That's exactly how the TikTok litigation was framed — by US creators and the US-incorporated entity, not by ByteDance Beijing.

So the realistic taxonomy: disclosure and labeling (settled, constitutional), personnel/visa/foreign-mission squeezes (constitutional, already used), distribution bans justified by ownership and national-security conduct (viable but needs a non-propaganda rationale to survive, per the TikTok ruling), and a flat content ban on "Chinese propaganda" as such (almost certainly dead under *Lamont* absent a dramatic doctrinal shift). The honest legislative answer to "what would have to change": amend Berman, or pass a PAFACA-clone and hope a court accepts a covert-influence/foreign-agent rationale the Supreme Court pointedly sidestepped last year.

Worth noting for a ChinaTalk angle: the asymmetry is that the most effective de-platforming of foreign state media in the US (RT, 2022) required zero legislation — it was carriage and app-store decisions. The pressure-campaign path is legally frictionless in a way the statutory path isn't.
