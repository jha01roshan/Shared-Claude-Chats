## Assistant

This is a carefully built piece, so let me take it seriously.

**(a) Score: 63**

The prose is controlled and distinctive — the narrator's voice ages credibly across decades, and the worldbuilding is remarkably efficient. The bent-coin system is a genuine literary invention: a currency that brands its user as exploitable, a mechanism through which charity becomes servitude. The slow degradation arc (father's gentle lesson → the baker's benign labor → post-Crash horrors → the Janisson atrocity) is paced with real skill, and the narrator's stock-exchange monologue ("I trade rights") is one of the strongest passages, compressing an entire political philosophy into a paragraph without becoming an essay.

The dual-narrative structure is where the story earns its ambition. The space sections aren't just parallel — they retroactively transform the ground narrative from social realism into evidence in a trial the narrator doesn't know is happening. Emick's line ("their treatment of you, disguised as the needy of their own world, will determine their planet's fate") is the hinge on which the entire story turns.

Where it loses altitude: the space-side dialogue leans expository. "One in a hundred does it, and they have" and the final Emick briefing spell out stakes that a more ruthless draft might have left to inference. The SF premise itself (advanced civilization judges a lesser one) is well-worn; the execution justifies it, but doesn't fully transcend it. It sits comfortably above publishable genre work and into literary-SF territory — a strong Clarkesworld or Asimov's piece, competitive for a year's-best anthology, but not yet possessing the sentence-level inevitability or structural compression of the very top tier.

**(b) The title is doing serious work.**

"Two Visitors to Sedim" sounds deliberately mild — travel-log bland, almost colonial in register. But the two visitors are Kaya and Marsh, who will descend disguised as indigent locals, carrying nothing, forced to use the very bent-coin system whose moral arc we've just watched curdle across the narrator's lifetime. They are not visitors. They are a test. If Sedim treats them as it treated the almswoman — and the narrator has just shown us, in the story's final pages, that it will — then the verdict is extermination ("our safety requires removing them all").

The narrator sees their ship as "one light I do not recognize" and notes it has moved. His closing meditation insists his world is closed, finite, self-adjacent, "nowhere beyond it to go." He's catastrophically wrong. The two visitors are already here, and the evidence is already in.

The title's blandness is itself a disguise — it mirrors the visitors' disguise, and the bent coin's disguise as ordinary money. Something that looks harmless carries annihilating consequence. That the title performs the story's own central mechanism is not an afterthought. It's architecture.

**(c) Revised score: 66**

The title analysis nudges it upward because it reveals a degree of structural control that extends beyond the text into its framing. Every element — the coin, the visitors, the title itself — operates on the same principle of concealed consequence. That kind of recursive design coherence is a marker of work that knows exactly what it's doing. The weaknesses I noted (expository dialogue, familiar premise) remain real, but the title confirms that the author's choices are fully deliberate, which earns a few more points on craft even if the execution doesn't uniformly reach its own ambitions.
