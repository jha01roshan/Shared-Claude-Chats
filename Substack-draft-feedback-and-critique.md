- "it's own weight" → "its own weight" (possessive, no apostrophe). This one shows up a few times — "it's parents" in the bison section and "it's life" and "it's parents" in the grass-fed beef section should all be "its."

**2. Editorial Thoughts**

The piece's biggest strength is the honesty about your own psychology — the "I'm low empathy for animals and I still got here" framing is genuinely persuasive in a way that most animal welfare writing isn't. You're not performing virtue; you're reasoning yourself into an uncomfortable position in real time. That's compelling.

A few structural observations:

The piece does two pretty different things: the first half is a moral reckoning ("factory farming is torture and I've been ignoring it"), and the second half is a practical buyer's guide ("here's what you can eat"). The transition between them is abrupt. A single sentence bridging — something like "so if the goal is to stop participating in the torture, what's actually left?" — would help the reader follow you.

The tone shifts are mostly a feature, not a bug, but the paragraph starting "Also, our culture just so clearly doesn't care" feels like it's doing too many things at once. It's simultaneously an argument from social proof, a self-deprecating tangent about your childhood, a rant about pets, and a rhetorical question about why animal lovers aren't more alarmed. Consider splitting it or cutting the pet rant (which is funny but dilutes the point).

The "offsetting" paragraph is the most philosophically interesting part of the piece and it's buried pretty deep. The distinction you're drawing — between offsetting as accounting vs. offsetting as moral laundering when you're directly causing suffering — is a real insight. It deserves more room to breathe.

The buyer's guide section is useful but reads like a different post. Not saying cut it, but it might benefit from being explicitly flagged as "Part 2" or something, because the emotional arc of the first half kind of dissipates into logistics.

**3. Blind Spots and Counterarguments**

This is where I want to push hardest.

**The "net positive life" framework is doing a lot of load-bearing work and you haven't really defended it.** You're basically adopting a total utilitarian view where creating a life worth living is a moral good, and therefore eating that animal is good because your demand caused that life to exist. But this is a deeply contested position in population ethics. On a "prior existence" view (we only have obligations to beings that already exist, not to bring new beings into existence), the fact that a bison had a nice life doesn't make your eating it *good* — it just makes it *less bad* than factory farming. You might want to at least flag that "eating animals is a clear moral good" is only true under specific philosophical assumptions.

**You're not considering fish welfare at all beyond wild salmon.** Farmed fish (tilapia, farmed salmon, farmed shrimp) are a massive category and arguably among the worst in terms of suffering-per-calorie. If you're going to eat at restaurants or at friends' houses, fish will come up constantly and "wild salmon" is a narrow carve-out. What's your position on farmed fish?

**The "I'll start when I move" framing is more revealing than you seem to realize.** You acknowledge the dissonance but kind of wave at it. A sharper version of the piece would sit with this longer — the fact that convenience can override a genuine moral conviction you hold *right now* is itself interesting data about how moral reasoning works in practice. You're basically demonstrating the exact mechanism by which everyone else also fails to act on this.

**Dairy and eggs from conventional sources get one sentence and deserve more.** You say store-bought milk and eggs involve torture, but then the buyer's guide only addresses eggs (local pasture-raised) and doesn't mention dairy at all. Are you giving up cheese? Butter? Yogurt? This is probably the single biggest practical gap in the piece.

**The "would I take this deal" thought experiment for New Zealand lamb is interesting but has a problem.** You're imagining the choice as "this life vs. no life," but the actual counterfactual for reducing lamb consumption isn't "this lamb never exists" on a one-to-one basis — it's a marginal reduction in future production. More importantly, you're applying your own preferences (as a human with rich cognitive life, future plans, relationships) to an animal whose subjective experience you can't access. The asymmetry is worth acknowledging.

**You don't address the environmental angle at all.** Even if an animal lives a happy life, grass-fed beef in particular has a significantly larger land and emissions footprint than factory-farmed beef. If you care about human welfare (which you say has been your primary frame), the climate and land-use impacts of scaling grass-fed beef are relevant. Some people argue that the environmental harm of grass-fed beef causes more total suffering (through climate effects on humans and ecosystems) than factory farming does directly.

**The certification question is underdeveloped.** You list some certifications (AGA, PCO, AGW, GAP 4+) in footnote 13 but don't explain them in the text, and you yourself note that people can just lie about labels. How confident are you that these certifications actually track what you care about? The gap between "certified grass-fed" and "actually lived a good life" might be bigger than you think, especially once you're buying at a grocery store rather than a farmers market.

**What about insects and crustaceans?** You carve out bivalves as non-sentient, but lobster, crab, and shrimp are in a very different category. And insect-based proteins are becoming more common. Where do these fall?

**The social cost framing in the opening is slightly defensive in a way that might undercut you.** Starting with "the Bay Area will judge me" rather than "I investigated this because it matters" makes it sound more strategic than genuine, even though the rest of the piece makes clear it's genuine. Consider whether the nerd-snipe framing in footnote 1 is a better opening hook than the social cost angle.
