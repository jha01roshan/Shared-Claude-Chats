Based on what's stated in the article, the board's decision to fire Sam Altman appears both justified and necessary from their perspective, though clearly poorly executed in terms of preparation and communication.

The article outlines several concerning patterns that eroded the board's trust:

1. Altman repeatedly misrepresented information to the board, including falsely claiming all three GPT-4 enhancements had safety approval when only one did

2. He failed to inform the board about Microsoft launching GPT-4 in India without safety approval, even during a six-hour meeting

3. He apparently owned OpenAI's Startup Fund personally without proper disclosure to the board

4. He allegedly created toxic management dynamics, pitting senior employees against each other 

5. He was caught in what appeared to be a direct lie about board member McCauley supposedly wanting Toner removed

The board members were acting in accordance with their fiduciary duty to the nonprofit that governed OpenAI. They had documentation of concerning behavior from Murati (the CTO) and Sutskever (chief scientist and co-founder), who both apparently felt Altman's leadership was problematic enough to warrant his removal.

However, the board critically failed by:

1. Not securing key stakeholder support before acting (especially Microsoft)
2. Not having a clear communication plan about the reasons for the firing
3. Not anticipating the employee revolt, including from the very executives who had provided evidence against Altman
4. Not being prepared to explain their decision when it inevitably became a global news story

The irony is that the executives who provided evidence against Altman, including Murati and Sutskever, ultimately sided with him - suggesting the board wasn't wrong about the problems, but was wrong about how deeply loyal the organization was to Altman despite those problems.
