## Assistant

**Communism, in its theoretical Marxist form (the higher stage of a classless, stateless society), fundamentally rejects and does not recognize implied contract law** because it views all contract law—including its "implied" facets—as a bourgeois legal superstructure tied to commodity production, private property, and individual market exchange, which communism abolishes entirely.

### What Implied Contract Law Entails (in Bourgeois/Capitalist Systems)
Implied contract law has two main facets in common-law traditions:
- **Implied-in-fact contracts**: These arise from the parties' conduct or circumstances, showing mutual assent and intent to be bound (e.g., a customer eating at a restaurant implies agreement to pay the bill).
- **Implied-in-law (quasi-contracts)**: These are court-imposed obligations to prevent **unjust enrichment**, even without any real agreement or intent (e.g., if someone mistakenly pays your debt, you may owe restitution).

These rest on core assumptions: autonomous legal subjects (individuals or entities with private wills and property rights), voluntary (or inferable) exchange of equivalents, and state coercion to enforce "fairness" or equity in private dealings.

### Marxist/Communist Theory of Law and Why Contracts (Including Implied) Are Incompatible
Marxist legal theorists, especially **Evgeny Pashukanis** in *The General Theory of Law and Marxism* (1924), explain law as the specific juridical form of **commodity-producing societies**. Law does not exist in a vacuum; it mirrors the economic base:
- Under capitalism, people relate as isolated "commodity owners" exchanging goods, services, or labor-power on the market. Contract law (express or implied) formalizes this as relations between equal, autonomous "legal subjects" whose wills meet in equivalent exchange.
- The labor contract itself is the archetype: the worker "freely" sells labor-power, but this masks exploitation (surplus value). Implied contracts extend this fiction—courts infer or impose "agreements" to keep the system running smoothly, protecting private property and preventing one party from being "unjustly" enriched at another's expense in a zero-sum market.
- Coercion appears neutral ("the authority of law") to sustain market peace, but it ultimately serves class domination.

In **communism** (post-socialist transition, when the state and classes have "withered away" per Marx and Engels):
- **Private property and commodity production end**. Means of production are socially owned; distribution follows "from each according to ability, to each according to need." There are no markets, no buying/selling between individuals, and no separate "owners" exchanging equivalents. Without commodities or private enrichment, there is nothing for contracts—implied or otherwise—to regulate.
- **No autonomous legal subjects**. Individuals are not atomized egoists with conflicting wills; social relations are direct and collective. "Agreements" are not inferred from conduct because production and allocation are planned and administered socially, not negotiated privately.
- **Law itself withers away**. As class antagonisms disappear, coercive legal norms (including enforcement of contracts or restitution) become unnecessary. Disputes are resolved through technical/administrative means or communal consensus, not judicial fictions like "implied assent" or "unjust enrichment." Pashukanis explicitly states that with the victory of a planned economy, "legal relations wither away" and enterprises lose their "legal personality," shifting to "technical-expediency ties."
- **Unjust enrichment has no basis**. In a society without private ownership, one person's "benefit" cannot come at another's expense in the propertied sense. Resources are allocated collectively; any imbalance is corrected through planning or social norms, not court-imposed quasi-contracts rooted in equity between private parties.

Thus, communism does not "recognize" these facets—it transcends them. Implied contract law presupposes the very things communism eliminates: individualism, private property, and the need for legal mediation of conflicting interests.

### Contrast with Transitional "Socialist" States (e.g., USSR)
In practice, during the socialist transition (what was called "building communism"), states like the USSR, China, or Eastern Bloc countries retained modified contract law:
- Contracts existed but were subordinated to the **state economic plan**. They were not based on private will or freedom of contract; the plan could impose, alter, or annul obligations.
- "Implied" elements appeared statutorily (e.g., rules on unjust enrichment in Czechoslovak codes), but these served socialist ownership and planning goals, not individual autonomy. Disputes went to arbitration to enforce plans, not courts protecting "implied" private deals.
- This was explicitly not "true" communism but a temporary form; full communism would eliminate even these.

Critics from libertarian or natural-law perspectives sometimes argue communism "violates" implied contracts (e.g., by nationalizing property without consent), but from the communist viewpoint, bourgeois implied contracts are themselves ideological tools upholding exploitation, with no validity in a post-capitalist society.

In short, communism does not recognize implied contract law because it rejects the entire framework of bourgeois private law. Contracts (implied or express) are historical relics of class society, replaced by conscious social administration in a harmonious, propertyless community. This is not a "bug" but the deliberate outcome of abolishing the economic conditions that make such legal fictions necessary.
