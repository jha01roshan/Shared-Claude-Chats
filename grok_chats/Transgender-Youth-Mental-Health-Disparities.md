## Assistant

**Yes, the data from large-scale population surveys, clinical studies, and systematic reviews consistently shows that children and adolescents who identify as transgender or gender-diverse (TGD) experience significantly higher rates of mental health conditions—including depression, anxiety, suicidality, and self-harm—compared to their cisgender peers.** This pattern holds across U.S. national surveys, clinic-referred samples, and international data, often with rates 2–10+ times higher depending on the outcome and comparison group.<grok:render card_id="c28e98" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">7</argument></grok:render>

### CDC 2023 Youth Risk Behavior Survey (YRBS)
This is a nationally representative sample of over 20,000 U.S. high school students (grades 9–12). About 3.3% identified as transgender and 2.2% as questioning. Key mental health and suicidality findings (adjusted prevalences):

- **Poor mental health in the past 30 days**: 64.9% of transgender students (vs. 17.8% cisgender males and 37.8% cisgender females); questioning students: 53.3%.
- **Persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness in the past 12 months** (a key marker for depressive symptoms): 71.9% of transgender students (vs. 26.0% cisgender males and 50.5% cisgender females); questioning: 68.9%.
- **Seriously considered attempting suicide in the past 12 months**: 52.9% of transgender students (vs. 12.1% cisgender males and 24.0% cisgender females); questioning: 44.9%.
- **Attempted suicide in the past 12 months**: 25.9% of transgender students (vs. 5.3% cisgender males and 11.0% cisgender females); questioning: 25.8%.<grok:render card_id="9d3fae" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">35</argument></grok:render>

These disparities were statistically significant and persisted after adjusting for grade and race/ethnicity. Transgender and questioning students also reported higher rates of bullying, violence victimization, and unstable housing, which align with the observed mental health gaps.<grok:render card_id="f9b1c9" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">7</argument></grok:render>

### Other Population-Based and Clinical Evidence
- A large Kaiser Permanente study (2006–2014) of TGD children and adolescents found mental health diagnoses were 3–13 times more common than in matched cisgender peers. For example, among TGD adolescents, depressive disorders affected 49% (transfeminine) and 62% (transmasculine); attention-deficit disorders were 15–16% in TGD children (3–7 times higher than cisgender reference groups). Suicidal ideation and self-inflicted injuries showed dramatically elevated prevalence ratios.<grok:render card_id="212b82" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">22</argument></grok:render>
- A 2022 study using the ABCD cohort (children aged 9–10) found TGD youth already showed higher parent-reported mental health problems and suicidality (14.8% vs. 2.9% in cisgender peers; adjusted odds ratio ~5.8 for suicidality).<grok:render card_id="48779a" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">20</argument></grok:render>
- Systematic reviews confirm the pattern. One review of 21 studies on gender-dysphoric adolescents/young adults (ages 14–27) found consistently higher rates of suicidal ideation (ranging 14–81% across measures), suicide attempts, self-harm, depression, and anxiety compared to cisgender peers. Both assigned-male-at-birth and assigned-female-at-birth subgroups showed elevated risks relative to cisgender boys and girls.<grok:render card_id="6e9bd9" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">5</argument></grok:render> Another broad review noted TGD youth experience more psychological distress than not only cisgender heterosexual peers but also cisgender sexual-minority peers.<grok:render card_id="0b857d" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">2</argument></grok:render>

Similar elevations appear in non-binary youth specifically (often comparable to or slightly higher than binary transgender youth on some metrics like general mental health and depressive symptoms) and in international clinic data (e.g., higher internalizing problems pre-treatment).<grok:render card_id="90d2e6" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">0</argument></grok:render>

These findings are robust across self-reported surveys, electronic health records, and clinic referrals, and they appear early (pre-pubertal ages) as well as in adolescence. The data do not address causation (e.g., minority stress, co-occurring conditions like autism, or other factors), but the raw prevalence disparities are well-documented and consistent.
