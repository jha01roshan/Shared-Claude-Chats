### Background
The term "anabasis" comes from Greek, meaning "going up" or "ascent," and historically refers to a march from the coast into the interior. The most well-known anabasis is Xenophon's account of the expedition led by Cyrus the Younger to seize the Persian throne from his brother, Artaxerxes II. This event involved Greek mercenaries and is detailed in Xenophon's work, "Anabasis," which covers both the march into Persia and the subsequent retreat.

### Timeline
The expedition began in March 401 BCE when the Greek mercenaries, led by Clearchus, set out from Sardis. They joined Cyrus's army and marched eastward, culminating in the Battle of Cunaxa on September 3, 401 BCE. After Cyrus's death in the battle, the Greeks faced a challenging retreat, reaching the Black Sea by March 399 BCE. ### Detailed Historical Context and Analysis

The anabasis, as described by Xenophon, is a pivotal event in ancient Greek history, often referred to as "The March of the Ten Thousand." This expedition is not only a military narrative but also a significant source for understanding Greek military tactics, geography, and cultural interactions in the Persian Empire during the 5th century BCE. Below, we explore the historical context, timeline, and broader implications, ensuring a comprehensive understanding for readers interested in ancient history.

#### Historical Context
Xenophon, an Athenian soldier and historian, documented the anabasis in his work, "Anabasis," composed around 370 BCE. The narrative centers on Cyrus the Younger, who, in 401 BCE, sought to overthrow his brother, Artaxerxes II, the Persian king. Cyrus enlisted approximately 10,000 Greek mercenaries, highlighting the growing influence of Greek military prowess in the region. This expedition is notable for its demonstration of Greek resilience, as the mercenaries, after Cyrus's death at the Battle of Cunaxa, had to navigate hostile territories to return home, a journey led in part by Xenophon himself.

The term "anabasis" itself, derived from Greek (ἀνάβασις), means "ascent" or "march inland from the coast," which aligns with the initial phase of the expedition from Sardis toward Babylon. However, Xenophon's work also covers the retreat, often termed "katabasis" (descent), creating some ambiguity in how "anabasis" is interpreted. For historical purposes, it primarily refers to the march into Persia, starting in 401 BCE.

#### Timeline and Key Events
The expedition's timeline is well-documented in historical sources, providing a clear framework for dating the anabasis:

- **March 401 BCE**: The Greek mercenaries, under Clearchus, began their march from Sardis, joining Cyrus's forces. This marks the start of the anabasis, as they moved eastward toward Babylonia, following the Euphrates River.
- **July 401 BCE**: The combined army reached Cayster, continuing their advance into Persian territory.
- **September 3, 401 BCE**: The Battle of Cunaxa occurred, approximately 40 miles from Babylon, where Cyrus was killed, and the Greek mercenaries initially succeeded against the Persian forces but found themselves leaderless and deep in enemy territory ([Battle of Cunaxa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cunaxa)).
- **Post-Battle Retreat**: Following Cunaxa, the Greeks elected new leaders, including Xenophon, and began their retreat northward. This phase, while part of Xenophon's narrative, is technically the katabasis, lasting until they reached the Black Sea in March 399 BCE.

Given the question's focus on "when was the anabasis," the year 401 BCE is most appropriate, as it encompasses the march into Persia, the primary definition of anabasis. However, for completeness, the entire expedition, including the retreat, spanned from 401 to 399 BCE, reflecting the complexity of the event.

#### Broader Implications and Comparisons
The anabasis is often compared to other historical expeditions, such as Arrian's "Anabasis of Alexander," which details Alexander the Great's campaigns from 334 to 323 BCE. While both share the term, they are distinct events, with Xenophon's anabasis being earlier and focused on Cyrus's failed coup. Another reference, the "Siberian Anabasis," relates to the Czech Legion's transit through Siberia during the Russian Civil War (early 20th century), but this is irrelevant to the ancient context.

Xenophon's account is valuable for its detailed descriptions of geography, military tactics, and intercultural interactions. For instance, the march through the Carduchi (modern-day Kurds) and Armenians provides insights into the challenges of traversing hostile terrains, a detail unexpected for readers unfamiliar with the depth of Xenophon's observations beyond military strategy.

The Anabasis of Xenophon, occurring in 401 BCE, fits into the history of Ancient Greece during the late Classical period, a time of significant transition following the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BCE). To understand its place, let’s situate it within the broader historical context of Ancient Greece, focusing on key events, cultural shifts, and its implications for Greek society and beyond.

### Timeline Context in Ancient Greek History
Ancient Greek history is typically divided into several periods: the Archaic (c. 1200–479 BCE), Classical (479–323 BCE), and Hellenistic (323–31 BCE). The Anabasis falls in the late Classical period, just after the end of the Peloponnesian War and before the rise of Macedonian power under Philip II and Alexander the Great. Here’s how it fits:

- **Archaic Period (c. 1200–479 BCE)**: This era saw the rise of city-states (poleis) like Athens and Sparta, the development of Greek colonization, and the Persian Wars (490–479 BCE), ending with Greek victories at Salamis and Plataea.
- **Classical Period (479–323 BCE)**:
  - **Early Classical (479–431 BCE)**: The "Golden Age" of Athens, marked by the Delian League, cultural flourishing (e.g., Pericles, Sophocles), and Persian retreat from Greek affairs.
  - **Peloponnesian War (431–404 BCE)**: A devastating conflict between Athens and Sparta, ending with Sparta’s victory and the decline of Athenian dominance.
  - **Post-War Period (404–323 BCE)**: The Anabasis (401 BCE) occurs here, during a time of Greek disunity, shifting alliances, and increasing interaction with Persia.
- **Hellenistic Period (323–31 BCE)**: Initiated by Alexander the Great’s conquests, spreading Greek culture across the Near East.

The Anabasis thus sits in a transitional phase between the Classical height and the Hellenistic expansion, reflecting a Greece that was fragmented yet militarily capable, with its mercenaries playing a role beyond its borders.

### Historical Placement and Significance
The Anabasis took place in 401 BCE, three years after the Peloponnesian War ended in 404 BCE with Sparta’s victory over Athens. This war left Greece politically unstable:
- Athens lost its empire and democracy (temporarily replaced by the oligarchic Thirty Tyrants in 404–403 BCE).
- Sparta emerged as the dominant power but struggled to maintain hegemony, facing internal dissent and external threats.
- Smaller city-states and mercenaries gained prominence as power vacuums emerged.

Against this backdrop, Cyrus the Younger, a Persian prince, hired around 10,000 Greek mercenaries to challenge his brother, Artaxerxes II, for the Persian throne. The expedition, starting in March 401 BCE and culminating in the Battle of Cunaxa (September 401 BCE), showcased Greek military prowess but also highlighted their detachment from homeland politics—many of these soldiers were veterans of the Peloponnesian War seeking employment.

After Cyrus’s death at Cunaxa, the Greeks’ retreat under Xenophon’s leadership (lasting until 399 BCE) demonstrated their resilience and adaptability, navigating hostile Persian territories back to the Black Sea. This event fits into Greek history as both a military and cultural milestone.

### Key Historical Connections
1. **Post-Peloponnesian War Instability (404–401 BCE)**:
   - The war’s end created a surplus of skilled soldiers with no unified cause, leading to mercenary service abroad. The Ten Thousand exemplify this trend, as noted by Xenophon himself, who joined partly for adventure and opportunity.
   - Sparta’s victory weakened Greek unity, allowing Persia to reassert influence over Greek affairs, a dynamic evident in Cyrus’s recruitment of Greeks against his brother.

2. **Greek-Persian Relations**:
   - The Anabasis occurred during the Achaemenid Empire’s height under Artaxerxes II (r. 404–358 BCE). While the Persian Wars (490–479 BCE) had established enmity, by 401 BCE, Greeks and Persians were increasingly entangled through trade, diplomacy, and mercenary work.
   - The success of the Ten Thousand in retreating through Persia exposed Persian vulnerabilities, influencing later Greek ambitions—most notably Alexander the Great’s conquests starting in 334 BCE.

3. **Lead-Up to Macedonian Ascendancy**:
   - The late 5th and early 4th centuries saw the decline of traditional polis power, setting the stage for Philip II of Macedon (r. 359–336 BCE) to unite Greece by 338 BCE at the Battle of Chaeronea.
   - Xenophon’s account of disciplined Greek soldiers succeeding against a vast empire foreshadowed Alexander’s campaigns, which capitalized on similar military strengths.

### Cultural and Intellectual Impact
The Anabasis also marks a shift in Greek intellectual history:
- **Xenophon’s Role**: A student of Socrates (who died in 399 BCE, shortly after the Anabasis concluded), Xenophon bridged the philosophical rigor of the Classical period with practical military narrative. His "Anabasis" contrasts with Thucydides’ analytical "History of the Peloponnesian War," offering a more personal, adventure-driven perspective.
- **Mercenary Identity**: The event reflects a growing professionalization of warfare, as Greek soldiers increasingly served foreign powers, a trend that intensified in the Hellenistic era.

### Broader Implications
The Anabasis fits into Ancient Greek history as a microcosm of its late Classical challenges and opportunities:
- **Military Prowess**: The Ten Thousand’s survival underscored Greek tactical superiority, boosting confidence in their ability to challenge larger empires—a precursor to Alexander’s success.
- **Political Fragmentation**: It highlights the lack of centralized Greek authority, with mercenaries acting independently of city-state directives.
- **Cultural Exchange**: The march through Persian lands (e.g., interactions with Carduchi and Armenians) enriched Greek geographic and ethnographic knowledge, influencing later Hellenistic cosmopolitanism.

### Comparison to Contemporary Events
In 401 BCE:
- Athens was rebuilding its democracy after the Thirty Tyrants (restored in 403 BCE).
- Sparta was negotiating the King’s Peace with Persia (387 BCE), a later treaty ceding Ionian cities to Persian control.
- The philosopher Plato was beginning his career, founding the Academy around 387 BCE, while Socrates’ execution in 399 BCE closed the Socratic era.

The Anabasis thus occurred amidst political recovery, philosophical transition, and shifting Greek-Persian dynamics, making it a pivotal moment in the late Classical narrative.

### Conclusion
The Anabasis of 401 BCE fits into Ancient Greek history as a late Classical event, postdating the Peloponnesian War and predating Macedonian unification. It reflects a Greece in flux—militarily skilled yet politically divided, with its citizens engaging the wider world as mercenaries. Its legacy, preserved in Xenophon’s writing, bridges the Classical polis-centric era and the expansive Hellenistic age, offering a lens into a transformative period that shaped Western history.
