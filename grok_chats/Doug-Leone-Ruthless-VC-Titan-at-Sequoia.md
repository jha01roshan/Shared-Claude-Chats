## Assistant

Doug Leone, who joined Sequoia Capital in 1988 and served as its global managing partner from 2012 until stepping back in 2022 (while remaining a general partner), built a legendary reputation as one of Silicon Valley's most successful and influential venture capitalists. Under his leadership, Sequoia expanded globally (including into China and India) and backed transformative companies like ServiceNow (now valued at $187B), Nubank ($73B), Wiz, Meraki, RingCentral, and Medallia (acquired for $6.4B). He's widely regarded as a "titan" of the industry—candid, intuitive, and committed to generational excellence—having mentored under Sequoia's founder Don Valentine and co-led the firm through booms and busts, including the dot-com crash. Interviews and profiles portray him as a high-performance coach who prioritizes "spiky" (intensely driven, sometimes abrasive) founders, simplicity in go-to-market strategies, and a team-oriented culture over "family" vibes. His net worth exceeds $6B, and he's praised for turning personal hardships (immigrating from Italy, enduring bullying) into empathy for resilient outliers.

Yes, Leone has a well-documented reputation for being ruthlessly capitalistic—prioritizing outsized returns, brutal selection in investments, and unyielding performance standards—while being tough (and occasionally overly abrasive) with underperforming founders. Early in his career, he was described as an "unguided missile": immature, belligerent, and "a pain in the ass" in boardrooms, carrying a hardscrabble upbringing's edge that made him "old school tough—maybe too tough." Sequoia founder Don Valentine once scolded him in a note: "Not fit to listen to founders," prompting Leone to evolve into a more charming, effective partner without losing his edge. He openly admits to a "super-tough side" where he "doesn't give an inch," viewing Sequoia as a "high-performance team" of "killers" rather than a forgiving family—mistakes are tolerated, but mediocrity isn't.

On underperforming founders specifically: Leone's style is decisive and direct, emphasizing support during creation and execution phases but drawing hard lines at persistent failure. He lets founders "create" early on, advising Sequoia to back off until product/market fit (PMF) is achieved: "If you get product/market fit, we can help you with everything else." But if PMF eludes them after 19 attempts ("the market has spoken"), it triggers a "different conversation": "It’s either him or her or all of us." This happens rarely (about 1 in 20 investments), and while Sequoia avoids reputational damage by "letting bygones be bygones," it can mean replacing the founder or CEO to salvage the company—classic VC realpolitik. Leone credits this ruthlessness with Sequoia's edge, but balances it with wartime loyalty: During the 2000 bust, he and partner Michael Moritz refused clawbacks, reinvested personally (writing ~100 checks), and ensured no limited partners lost money, calling it their "proudest moment" for doing "what nobody else would do."

Critics and anecdotes paint him as borderline brutal in execution—favoring "brutal selection" and "massive upside" over coddling—but admirers see it as tough love from a results-obsessed mentor who "winds up" founders by decoding their motivations over dinners. In today's softer VC landscape, he concedes his early abrasiveness "wouldn't cut it," yet it fueled Sequoia's dominance. Overall, his rep is one of respected ferocity: capitalistic to the core, tough on flops, but generous with second chances when the fight's still there.
