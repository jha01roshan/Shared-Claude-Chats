On September 26, 1960, Fidel Castro, the revolutionary leader and Prime Minister of Cuba, stepped onto the world stage at the United Nations General Assembly in New York City to deliver what would become one of the longest speeches in the organization's history. Clocking in at four hours and twenty-nine minutes, Castro’s address was a fiery, unapologetic critique of imperialism, capitalism, and, most pointedly, the United States, while also laying bare the growing tensions between his nascent revolutionary government and its powerful northern neighbor.

### The Setting
The year 1960 was a pivotal moment in the Cold War. The Cuban Revolution had triumphed just over a year earlier, in January 1959, when Castro’s forces overthrew the U.S.-backed dictator Fulgencio Batista. By 1960, Cuba was rapidly aligning itself with the Soviet Union, a move that alarmed the United States, which saw a communist foothold in the Western Hemisphere as a direct threat. Earlier that year, the U.S. had begun imposing economic sanctions on Cuba, and the CIA was already plotting covert operations to destabilize Castro’s regime—plans that would culminate in the failed Bay of Pigs invasion in 1961. Meanwhile, the United Nations, founded in 1945 to foster international cooperation, had become a battleground for ideological clashes between the capitalist West and the communist East.

Castro arrived in New York with a delegation of Cuban officials and a chip on his shoulder. The visit itself was fraught with tension. U.S. authorities restricted the Cuban delegation’s movements, and several Manhattan hotels refused to accommodate them, citing security concerns or political pressure. Eventually, Castro moved his entourage to the Hotel Theresa in Harlem, a predominantly Black neighborhood, where he was warmly received by local residents and met with figures like Malcolm X, signaling his intent to align Cuba with anti-imperialist and marginalized voices globally.

The General Assembly hall, packed with diplomats from around the world, buzzed with anticipation as Castro took the podium. Dressed in his signature olive-green military fatigues, he embodied the image of a revolutionary defiant against Western norms—right down to ignoring the UN’s informal time limits for speeches.

### The Speech
Castro’s marathon oration began with a broad condemnation of colonialism and imperialism, themes central to his revolutionary ideology. He accused the United States of exploiting Latin America for centuries and painted Cuba’s revolution as a necessary break from that history. But the heart of his speech was a searing indictment of U.S. policy toward Cuba. He detailed how American economic interests had dominated the island under Batista, leaving its people impoverished, and argued that the U.S. was now punishing Cuba for daring to assert its sovereignty.

He didn’t hold back on specifics. Castro criticized the U.S. for cutting Cuba’s sugar quota—a key economic lifeline—and accused American leaders of hypocrisy for preaching democracy while supporting dictatorships friendly to their interests. He also hinted at U.S. aggression, foreshadowing the hostilities that would soon escalate. Interspersed with these attacks were defenses of Cuba’s social reforms, like literacy campaigns and land redistribution, which he presented as models for other developing nations.

The speech wasn’t just a litany of grievances; it was a performance. Castro’s delivery was passionate, often extemporaneous, and laced with rhetorical flourishes that played to both the delegates in the room and the global audience beyond. He spoke at length about the plight of poor nations, aligning Cuba with the emerging Non-Aligned Movement, though his Soviet ties complicated that stance. At one point, he famously quipped about the UN’s time constraints, saying, “We come from a country where one can speak as long as necessary,” a jab at both American impatience and Cuba’s oral revolutionary tradition.

### The Reaction
The response in the hall was mixed. Some delegates, particularly from Latin American and newly independent African and Asian nations, cheered Castro’s boldness. Others, especially from the U.S. and its allies, sat in stony silence or walked out. American diplomats dismissed the speech as propaganda, with U.S. Secretary of State Christian Herter reportedly calling it “a marathon exercise in distortion.” Newspapers like *The New York Times* noted its length with a mix of awe and exasperation, while critics accused Castro of grandstanding.

Yet the speech had its intended effect. It solidified Castro’s image as a fearless opponent of U.S. hegemony and amplified Cuba’s voice on the world stage. For many in the developing world, it was a rallying cry against neocolonialism. For the U.S., it was a stark reminder of the challenge posed by Castro’s regime, just 90 miles from Florida.

### The Legacy
Castro’s 1960 UN address remains a record-breaker—still the longest ever delivered at the General Assembly—and a defining moment in his leadership. It encapsulated the defiance that would characterize U.S.-Cuba relations for decades, setting the stage for the Cuban Missile Crisis two years later and a Cold War standoff that outlasted the Soviet Union itself. In the annals of UN history, it stands as a testament to how a small island nation, through sheer audacity and verbosity, could command the world’s attention for over four hours on a September day in 1960.
