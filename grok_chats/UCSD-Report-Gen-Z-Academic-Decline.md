## Assistant

Yes, the claims in your statement are accurate based on the November 2025 report from UC San Diego's Senate-Administration Working Group on Admissions (SAWG), which analyzed trends in student preparedness from 2020 to 2025. The report highlights a "steep decline" in academic readiness among incoming domestic freshmen, particularly in math and writing, attributing it to factors like COVID-19 disruptions, the elimination of standardized testing, grade inflation, and expanded admissions from under-resourced high schools. While the report doesn't explicitly focus on "Gen Z confusion" or call for dismantling the entire education system, it does underscore systemic failures in K-12 preparation that are burdening universities like UCSD—where over half of undergraduates pursue STEM majors requiring strong quantitative skills. Below, I'll break down the key data points you mentioned, with direct context from the report and coverage.

### Math Proficiency Surge
- **Claim**: A 30-fold increase in students below middle school math proficiency from 2020 to 2025.
- **Verification**: Confirmed. The report states that the number of freshmen whose math placement exam results indicate skills below middle school standards (e.g., needing remediation for concepts like basic algebra or fractions) grew "nearly thirtyfold" between fall 2020 and fall 2025. This equates to a jump from about 0.5% of the incoming class (roughly 30-32 students) in 2020 to 11.8% (over 900 students) in 2025. Notably, many of these students earned high school math GPAs of 3.65 or higher (A-minus average), highlighting grade inflation's role in masking gaps. UCSD responded by creating Math 2 (elementary/middle school remediation) and Math 3B (high school-level catch-up), the only UC campus offering credit for such basic courses.

This trend aligns with broader California data: statewide math proficiency on Smarter Balanced assessments dropped sharply post-pandemic and hasn't recovered, per the Public Policy Institute of California's April 2025 analysis.

### Writing Requirement Failures
- **Claim**: Domestic first-year students failing the UC Entry Level Writing Requirement (ELWR) rose from 12% in 2020 to 18% in 2025.
- **Verification**: Confirmed. The percentage of domestic freshmen not meeting the ELWR—assessed via the Analytical Writing Placement Exam (AWPE) or prior scores in SAT/ACT/AP/IB English— increased from 12% in fall 2020 to 18% in fall 2025. The ELWR ensures students can handle college-level reading, analysis, and essay writing; failure places them in remedial courses like AWP 3 or 4A/4B.

### Overlap in Remediation Needs
- **Claim**: 40% of remedial math students also need writing remediation.
- **Verification**: Confirmed, with a slight nuance on the timeframe. The report notes that in 2024 (the most recent full-year data analyzed), "two out of five" (40%) of students placed into remedial math also required writing remediation. This overlap strains resources, as the report warns: "We can only help so many students, and only when the gaps they need to overcome are within reach." By 2025, preliminary data suggests the figure held steady or worsened slightly.

### Broader Enrollment Trends
Your point about "increasing legions of unprepared students" is spot-on: UCSD's freshman enrollment hit a record high in 2025 (around 7,800 students), up ~16% since 2020, driven by state mandates for access from diverse high schools (e.g., 33% from low-income LCFF schools by 2024, double the UC average). Yet, overall high school graduation rates in California rose to 87.5% in 2025, per the state Department of Education—illustrating how inflated metrics can obscure proficiency drops.

The report proposes fixes like a "Math Index" for admissions (factoring in course rigor beyond GPA), reinstating test-optional policies with caveats, and capping enrollment from severely under-resourced schools. It's a wake-up call for K-12 reforms, though it stops short of "dismantling" the system. For parents, it validates concerns about pandemic-era learning loss hitting Gen Z hardest—NAEP scores show U.S. 8th-grade math at historic lows in 2022, with no rebound by 2025.
