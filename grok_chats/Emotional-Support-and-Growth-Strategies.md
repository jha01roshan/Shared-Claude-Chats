If you've tried everything.........and still feel numb. READ THIS!!!

If you’ve been doing the work. The therapy, the journaling, the breathing, the meds, the shadow work, the damn gratitude lists and you still feel empty, flat, or numb…I just want to say: it’s not your fault.

There’s nothing wrong with you. But there might be something wrong with the approach.

Because most healing tools? They try to fix what you feel. But they rarely touch what you believe.

And if your subconscious is quietly running beliefs like:

* “I’m not safe to feel good.”  
* “I always ruin good things.”  
* “People leave when I open up.”  
* “Nothing ever works out for me.”  
* “If I get my hopes up, I’ll get hurt.”  

Then it makes total sense that you’d be emotionally flatlining. Not because you’re broken, but because your system is doing exactly what it was trained to do: protect you.



**Numbness Isn’t Apathy. It’s Survival.**

That blank feeling you can’t explain. The one that isn’t sadness or anger or grief, just... nothing, isn’t laziness. It’s not you “giving up.” It’s your system pulling the emergency brake.

When life has been full of chaos, rejection, trauma, or disappointment, your subconscious starts learning:

“Feeling too much is dangerous.” “Excitement leads to pain.” “It’s safer not to hope.”

So, weirdly, it starts protecting you by turning the volume down. On everything. Not just fear and anxiety, but also joy, creativity, motivation, and connection.

And you end up stuck in this gray zone where you’re technically “here,” but it doesn’t feel like you’re living.



**Why Typical Advice Doesn’t Work for This**

If you tell someone you feel numb, they’ll probably give you the usual advice:

* “Try going outside more.”  
* “Make a routine.”  
* “Exercise helps.”  
* “Get on meds if you’re not already.”  
* “Maybe talk to a therapist again?”  
* “Just keep pushing through.”  

All well-meaning suggestions. And honestly, some of it can help. But if you’ve been there you know the truth:

You can be doing everything right and still feel nothing.

Because the real block isn’t your habits or your willpower, it’s the invisible belief system your subconscious is still running in the background.

Even if you consciously want to feel better. If your core programming says “it’s not safe to feel” or “I don’t deserve good things,” your brain and body will sabotage your healing without you even realizing it.

You’ll procrastinate. You’ll pull away from people. You’ll stop replying. You’ll talk yourself out of even trying. And then you’ll beat yourself up for doing it again.

It’s a trap. And it sucks. And most people have no idea they’re even in it.



**So What’s the Fix?**

Not another journal prompt. Not another app that sends you a reminder to “breathe.” Not forcing yourself to write down what you're grateful for when you feel numb.

The fix is going to the root.

The root isn’t your schedule, your brain chemistry, or your phone addiction.

The root is the belief system your subconscious created at some earlier point in your life when it decided what was “safe,” what was “possible,” and what role you were supposed to play in the world.

Beliefs like:

* “I always mess things up.”  
* “It’s better not to care too much.”  
* “People don’t really see me.”  
* “I’m too much.”  
* “I’m not enough.”  
* “Good things don’t last.”  

These beliefs become your inner operating system. And they shape everything, from your thoughts and emotions, to your decisions, your relationships, even the way you try to heal.

So if your system still believes that being alive = being vulnerable = getting hurt, it will protect you by keeping you numb.



**What Happens When You Rewrite the Programming?**

Here’s the part most people never get to experience because no one tells them it’s possible:

When those beliefs are gone, things stop feeling so hard. Not because life gets easier, but because the constant battle inside your head finally shuts off.

The war between “I want to change” and “I always fail anyway” ends. The guilt for not being productive fades. The shame about why you can’t just be “normal” softens. And for the first time in maybe a long time… you can feel without it taking you out.

You don’t have to fake joy. You don’t have to chase motivation. You don’t have to force connection. It just starts showing up again because your system isn’t fighting it anymore.

That’s the difference between managing your symptoms and releasing the source.

And no one ever talks about it.



**This Isn’t About Thinking Positive**

Let’s be clear: This isn’t about replacing “bad thoughts” with “positive affirmations.”

Your subconscious isn’t dumb. It knows when you’re lying.

If you slap a fake “I am confident and happy” over a foundation of “I always screw things up,” your system won’t buy it.

The goal isn’t to lie to yourself. It’s to let go of the lies someone else put there.

You don’t need to convince yourself you’re healed. You just need to stop carrying the belief that healing isn’t possible for you.

That’s when your system starts to update. That’s when numbness fades—not overnight, but steadily. That’s when you can finally start to feel again, not everything all at once, but enough to make you want to keep going.



**Final Thought**

If you’ve tried everything and still feel stuck, lease don’t take that as proof that something’s wrong with you.

It’s just proof that no one’s shown you how to go deep enough. Not to your past, not to your flaws but to your wiring.

You are not broken. You are not defective. You are not unfixable.

You’re just running a system that was designed to help you survive, not to help you feel.

And now… it’s time to upgrade it.

Not by working harder. Not by trying to be more positive. But by letting go of the beliefs that never belonged to you in the first place.

You can rewrite the story. You can reconnect to your own aliveness. And one day soon you’ll realize that numb wasn’t the end.

It was just the part of you that went quiet until you were finally safe enough to come home to yourself.

The blog is spot-on that beliefs—those core, often unconscious assumptions about yourself and the world—shape how you feel and act more than surface-level thoughts do. Changing beliefs takes time and patience, but it’s absolutely possible. Here’s a step-by-step approach to help you go to the root, as the blog suggests, and start updating that inner operating system.

---

### 1. Identify the Core Beliefs Holding You Back
Before you can change a belief, you need to know what it is. These beliefs often hide in the background, so they can be tricky to spot. They might show up as automatic thoughts or patterns in how you react to situations.

**How to do this:**
- **Reflect on your numbness or stuck points.** When you feel flat, disconnected, or like you’re sabotaging yourself, pause and notice what’s happening in your mind. For example, if you avoid reaching out to a friend, what’s the underlying fear or assumption? Maybe it’s “They won’t really care” or “I’ll be too much for them.”
- **Look for recurring themes.** The blog lists examples like “I’m not safe to feel good” or “I always ruin things.” Write down moments when you’ve felt stuck or numb and ask, “What must I believe about myself or the world for this to keep happening?” For instance, if you procrastinate on something meaningful, you might uncover a belief like “I’ll fail anyway, so why try?”
- **Use gentle curiosity.** Instead of forcing yourself to “figure it out,” approach this like you’re exploring a story. You might say to yourself, “I wonder why I feel so guarded about hope—what’s my system trying to protect me from?”

**Example:** Let’s say you feel numb when you think about pursuing a goal, like a creative project. You might notice thoughts like “It won’t work out” or “I’m not good enough.” Dig deeper: those thoughts might stem from a belief like “Good things don’t happen for me” or “I don’t deserve success.”

---

### 2. Trace the Belief’s Origin (Without Getting Stuck There)
Beliefs often form in response to past experiences—maybe a time when you were hurt, rejected, or overwhelmed. Understanding where a belief came from can help you see it’s not a “truth” about you but a survival strategy from a specific moment.

**How to do this:**
- **Ask yourself, “When did I first feel this way?”** You don’t need a perfect answer, but think about early moments when this belief might have taken root. For example, if you believe “I’m not safe to feel good,” maybe you recall a time when expressing joy led to criticism or disappointment.
- **Acknowledge the context.** Recognize that the belief made sense back then. If you grew up in a chaotic environment, believing “It’s safer not to hope” might have protected you from constant letdown. This isn’t about blaming your past but about seeing the belief as a coping mechanism, not a fact.
- **Keep it light.** You don’t need to relive trauma or dig into every detail. The goal is to loosen the belief’s grip by realizing it’s tied to a specific time, not your whole life.

**Example:** If you believe “People leave when I open up,” you might recall a childhood friendship that ended abruptly when you shared something vulnerable. Seeing this can help you realize the belief was a way to protect yourself from rejection, not a universal rule.

---

### 3. Challenge the Belief with Evidence and Compassion
Once you’ve identified a belief, you can start to question its validity. This isn’t about forcing positive thinking (as the blog wisely warns against) but about gently testing whether the belief still holds true.

**How to do this:**
- **Look for counterevidence.** Ask, “Is this belief always true? Are there times when it hasn’t been?” For example, if you believe “I always mess things up,” think of moments when you’ve succeeded, even in small ways—like finishing a task, helping a friend, or showing up for yourself.
- **Talk to the belief like a friend.** Imagine the belief is a scared part of you trying to keep you safe. You might say, “I get why you’re here, but I’m in a different place now. I want to try something new.” This softens the resistance and makes change feel safer.
- **Start small.** You don’t need to overhaul the belief overnight. If you believe “Good things don’t last,” look for tiny moments where good things *did* last, even briefly—like a pleasant conversation or a day that felt okay.

**Example:** For the belief “I’m not safe to feel good,” you might recall a time when you felt a spark of joy (maybe listening to music or laughing with someone) and nothing bad happened. This plants a seed that feeling good doesn’t always lead to pain.

---

### 4. Practice New Beliefs Through Action
Beliefs don’t just change by thinking differently—they shift when you act in ways that align with a new story. This is where the “rewriting” happens. You’re teaching your subconscious that it’s safe to let go of the old belief by behaving as if the new one is true.

**How to do this:**
- **Choose a small, safe experiment.** If you believe “I’m not enough,” try one action that assumes you *are* enough—like sharing an idea in a low-stakes setting or treating yourself to something small without guilt.
- **Celebrate tiny wins.** Every time you act against the old belief, acknowledge it. For example, if you reach out to someone despite fearing rejection, say to yourself, “I showed up, and that’s proof I’m capable of connection.”
- **Use “yet” or “learning” language.** Instead of “I’m not good at this,” try “I’m learning how to do this.” This creates space for a new belief to grow without feeling fake.

**Example:** If you believe “People don’t really see me,” experiment by sharing something small about yourself with someone you trust. If they respond positively (even a little), it’s evidence that you *can* be seen. Over time, these actions build a new belief: “I am worthy of being noticed.”

---

### 5. Support Your Nervous System While You Rewire
The blog mentions numbness as a survival response, which means your nervous system plays a big role in keeping old beliefs in place. To change beliefs, you need to create a sense of safety so your system doesn’t default to “shut down” mode.

**How to do this:**
- **Practice grounding techniques.** Simple things like slow breathing (inhale for 4, hold for 4, exhale for 6), feeling your feet on the ground, or sipping cold water can help your body feel safe enough to process change.
- **Check in with your body.** When you challenge a belief, notice if you feel tense, heavy, or anxious. This is your system reacting to the “threat” of change. Pause, breathe, and remind yourself, “I’m safe to try this.”
- **Be patient with numbness.** If you feel flat while working on this, that’s okay. It’s your system adjusting. Instead of fighting it, say, “This is my body doing its job. I’m learning to feel again, step by step.”

**Example:** If you’re working on the belief “It’s better not to care too much,” you might feel anxious when you try caring about a new hobby. Ground yourself by focusing on your breath, then take a tiny step (like spending 5 minutes on the hobby) to show your system it’s safe to care.

---

### 6. Seek Support to Go Deeper
Changing beliefs can be hard to do alone, especially if they’re tied to deep pain or trauma. The blog hints at this by saying “no one’s shown you how to go deep enough.” A trusted guide can help you navigate this process with care.

**How to do this:**
- **Consider therapy modalities that target beliefs.** Approaches like Internal Family Systems (IFS), EMDR, or somatic therapy focus on rewiring subconscious patterns, not just managing symptoms. A therapist trained in these can help you access and release old beliefs.
- **Talk to safe people.** Share your journey with a friend or support group who can validate your efforts and remind you that you’re not “broken.”
- **Be kind to yourself.** If you hit resistance or feel stuck, it’s not a sign you’re failing. It’s a sign you’re touching something real. Keep going at your own pace.

**Example:** If you’re working on “I don’t deserve good things,” a therapist might help you explore where that belief started and guide you through exercises to feel deserving in your body, not just your mind.

---

### Putting It All Together
Let’s say you’ve identified a belief like “I always ruin good things.” Here’s how you might apply these steps:
1. **Identify:** Notice when this belief shows up (e.g., when you avoid celebrating a win because you’re waiting for something to go wrong).
2. **Trace:** Reflect on when you first felt this way—maybe a time when a happy moment was followed by disappointment.
3. **Challenge:** Look for times when you *didn’t* ruin things, like when you completed a task or made someone smile. Tell yourself, “I’m allowed to have good moments.”
4. **Act:** Take a small risk, like celebrating a small success (e.g., treating yourself to coffee). Notice how it feels to let good things happen.
5. **Support your system:** If you feel numb or anxious, use grounding techniques to stay present. Remind yourself, “I’m safe to feel this.”
6. **Seek help:** If the belief feels too heavy, work with a therapist to explore its roots and release it.
