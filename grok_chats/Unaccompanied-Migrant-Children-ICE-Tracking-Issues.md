## Human

How many children has ICE lost at the border?

---

## Assistant

There is no definitive number of children that U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) has "lost" at the border, as the term "lost" can be misleading and lacks a uniform definition across reports. However, several sources provide data on unaccompanied migrant children (UCs) whose locations ICE could not account for after their release from custody, based on recent audits and investigations.

A 2024 report from the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) Office of Inspector General (OIG) found that, between fiscal years 2019 and 2023, more than 32,000 unaccompanied migrant children failed to appear for their scheduled immigration court hearings, and ICE was unable to monitor their locations after they were released from federal custody, typically to the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS). The report also noted that ICE did not issue Notices to Appear (NTAs) to over 291,000 additional UCs, meaning these children were not placed in removal proceedings or assigned court dates. While some interpret this to suggest a total of up to 323,000 children could be unaccounted for, the audit does not explicitly state that all 291,000 without NTAs are "lost"—only that ICE lacks a formal process to track them post-release.

It’s important to clarify that ICE’s role is to transfer unaccompanied children to HHS, which then places them with sponsors, often family members, in the United States. After this handoff, ICE does not typically retain responsibility for tracking these children unless they are in removal proceedings. The OIG report highlighted systemic issues, such as inadequate staffing and lack of automated information-sharing processes, which hinder ICE’s ability to monitor these children’s locations after release. However, immigration experts caution that "lost" does not necessarily mean these children are missing or in danger—many may be living with sponsors, attending school, or pursuing legal relief like asylum, but ICE lacks updated records of their whereabouts.

Claims of higher numbers, such as 325,000 or more, often stem from combining the 32,000 who missed court dates with the 291,000 who were not issued NTAs, but this aggregation has been criticized as misleading since the latter group’s status is not confirmed as "lost." Additionally, a separate figure of 85,000 children "lost" by HHS (not ICE) has been cited, originating from a 2023 New York Times report about children HHS could not contact for follow-up welfare checks after placement with sponsors. This is distinct from ICE’s figures and reflects a different stage of the process.

In summary, the most concrete number from recent data is that ICE could not account for the locations of more than 32,000 unaccompanied migrant children who missed court hearings between 2019 and 2023. The potential for a higher figure—up to 323,000—exists if including those without NTAs, but this is speculative without further evidence. The situation reflects challenges in tracking rather than a literal loss of physical custody at the border.
