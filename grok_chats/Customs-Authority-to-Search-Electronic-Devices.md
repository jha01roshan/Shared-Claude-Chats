### Absolutely Forbidden from Reading/Using Devices
These countries have legal or constitutional protections that generally prohibit customs or immigration officials from inspecting electronic devices or reading data without significant justification, such as a court order or exceptional circumstances. Routine searches without cause are typically not allowed.

- **European Union (EU) Member States (e.g., Germany, France, Spain)**  
  - **Reason**: The EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and strong privacy laws, combined with the European Convention on Human Rights, impose strict limits on searches of personal data. Customs officials can inspect physical goods, but searching electronic devices usually requires a warrant or specific suspicion of a crime (e.g., trafficking or terrorism). For example, Germany’s Federal Constitutional Court has emphasized privacy rights, restricting random or suspicionless searches of digital devices at borders.
  - **Conditions**: Searches are rare and typically need judicial oversight or probable cause beyond mere border entry.
  - **Caveat**: Practices vary slightly by country, and national security exceptions (e.g., terrorism) may override in specific cases, but this is not routine.

- **Iceland, Norway, Switzerland (Non-EU Schengen Area)**  
  - **Reason**: Similar to the EU, these countries adhere to strong privacy frameworks under the Schengen Agreement and domestic laws. Random or blanket searches of electronic devices are generally prohibited without a legal basis like a warrant.
  - **Conditions**: Inspections require specific justification, often tied to criminal investigations rather than routine border checks.

- **Japan**  
  - **Reason**: Japan’s constitution (Article 35) protects against unreasonable searches, and privacy is highly valued. Customs officials focus on physical goods (e.g., contraband) rather than digital content unless there’s clear evidence of illegal activity (e.g., child exploitation material).
  - **Conditions**: Searches of devices would typically require a court order or strong suspicion, not just border authority.

### May Under Certain Conditions
These countries allow customs or immigration officials to inspect electronic devices, but only under specific circumstances, such as suspicion of illegal activity, national security concerns, or with procedural safeguards. Random or unrestricted searches are not the norm.

- **Canada**  
  - **Authority**: The Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) can search electronic devices under the Customs Act, but a 2023 Federal Court ruling (R. v. Pike) established that such searches require "reasonable grounds to suspect" a violation of customs or immigration laws. Random searches without cause are not permitted.
  - **Conditions**: Officers must articulate a specific reason (e.g., suspected smuggling or illegal content). Devices can be detained briefly, but prolonged retention or data copying requires further justification.
  - **Limits**: The Supreme Court of Canada has emphasized privacy rights, so searches are not unlimited.

- **United Kingdom**  
  - **Authority**: Under the Terrorism Act 2000 and Immigration Act 1971, UK Border Force officials can examine devices, but this is tied to specific triggers like terrorism, smuggling, or immigration violations.
  - **Conditions**: Searches require reasonable suspicion in most cases, though the threshold can be low at borders. The Investigatory Powers Act 2016 adds oversight for data retention or deeper forensic searches.
  - **Limits**: Random searches are rare, and privacy laws (e.g., UK GDPR) impose some constraints.

- **New Zealand**  
  - **Authority**: The Customs Service can inspect devices under the Customs and Excise Act 2018, but a 2018 policy update requires "reasonable cause to suspect" an offense (e.g., importing prohibited material).
  - **Conditions**: Travelers must unlock devices if asked, or face fines (up to NZD 5,000), but searches are not routine. Data retention is limited to the purpose of the search.
  - **Limits**: Privacy Act 2020 provides some protections against arbitrary intrusion.

- **South Korea**  
  - **Authority**: Customs officials can inspect devices under the Customs Act, but this is typically linked to specific concerns like smuggling or intellectual property violations.
  - **Conditions**: Searches require suspicion of a violation, and South Korea’s Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA) limits unnecessary data access.
  - **Limits**: Routine digital searches are uncommon, and privacy laws apply.

### Unlimited Authority
These countries grant customs or immigration officials broad, often unrestricted authority to inspect electronic devices and read messages/data at borders, with little to no requirement for suspicion or oversight. Refusal to comply can lead to denial of entry, device seizure, or penalties.

- **United States**  
  - **Authority**: U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) has plenary authority under 19 CFR 162.6 to search all persons, baggage, and merchandise—including electronic devices—at the border without a warrant or suspicion. The Supreme Court has upheld this under the "border search exception" to the Fourth Amendment.
  - **Scope**: CBP can conduct "basic" searches (manual review) without cause and "advanced" searches (forensic analysis) with reasonable suspicion. Travelers can be asked to unlock devices; refusal may lead to detention, device seizure, or denial of entry (for non-citizens).
  - **Limits**: Policies exist (e.g., CBP Directive 3340-049A) to ensure professionalism, but legal challenges (e.g., Alasaad v. McAleenan, 2019) have not significantly curtailed this power.

- **Australia**  
  - **Authority**: The Australian Border Force (ABF) can search devices under the Customs Act 1901, with no requirement for suspicion. The 2018 Assistance and Access Act further enables authorities to compel access to encrypted devices.
  - **Scope**: ABF can copy data, and refusal to unlock devices can result in seizure or fines. Searches are routine for some travelers.
  - **Limits**: Few practical restrictions; privacy laws like the Privacy Act 1988 have limited impact at borders.

- **China**  
  - **Authority**: Chinese customs and immigration officials have broad powers under national security and customs laws to inspect devices, especially in sensitive regions like Xinjiang.
  - **Scope**: Authorities can demand passwords, install monitoring apps (e.g., in Xinjiang), and retain data. Refusal can lead to detention or entry denial.
  - **Limits**: Virtually none; privacy protections are minimal, and state security trumps individual rights.

- **Russia**  
  - **Authority**: Federal Customs Service and border guards can inspect devices under customs and security laws, with no clear requirement for suspicion.
  - **Scope**: Searches can include data review, and refusal may result in entry denial or device confiscation. National security is often cited.
  - **Limits**: Constitutional privacy rights exist but are weakly enforced at borders.

- **India**  
  - **Authority**: Customs officials under the Customs Act 1962 can search devices without suspicion, bolstered by IT Act 2000 provisions for security-related data access.
  - **Scope**: Officers can demand access to devices; refusal may lead to seizure or entry issues. Searches are common for certain travelers.
  - **Limits**: Courts have occasionally pushed back (e.g., privacy rulings like Puttaswamy, 2017), but border authority remains broad.

### Notes and Observations
- **Trends**: Countries with strong privacy laws (e.g., EU, Japan) lean toward "absolutely forbidden" or "conditional" categories, while those prioritizing security or with weaker democratic oversight (e.g., China, Russia) fall under "unlimited authority."
- **Ambiguity**: Some nations (e.g., Brazil, South Africa) lack clear public policies on device searches, making categorization difficult. I’ve excluded them due to insufficient data.
- ** Traveler Status**: Citizens often face fewer consequences (e.g., can’t be denied entry) than non-citizens, who risk refusal across all categories.
- **Practicality**: Even in "unlimited authority" countries, searches aren’t always routine due to resource constraints, but the legal power exists.
