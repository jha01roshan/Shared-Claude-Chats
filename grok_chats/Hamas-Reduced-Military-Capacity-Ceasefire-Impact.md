### Current Status of Hamas's Military Capabilities

As of October 10, 2025, Hamas's ability to wage war against Israel has been dramatically curtailed following nearly two years of intense conflict that began with the October 7, 2023, attacks. The group, once a formidable militant force with an estimated 24 battalions and sophisticated tunnel networks, has been reduced to a fragmented entity with limited operational capacity. This degradation stems from Israel's sustained military operations, which have dismantled much of Hamas's infrastructure, eliminated key leadership, and restricted its access to resources. However, Hamas retains a political and insurgent presence in Gaza, and a fragile ceasefire agreement—brokered by U.S. President Donald Trump and announced on October 8—has temporarily halted active hostilities, shifting focus to disarmament and reconstruction.

#### Key Aspects of Hamas's Degraded Military Posture
- **Loss of Conventional Fighting Strength**: Israeli assessments and independent analyses indicate that Hamas's military wing, the Izz ad-Din al-Qassam Brigades, has been decimated. Pre-war estimates placed its fighters at 30,000–40,000; by mid-2025, this had shrunk to a few thousand, with most organized units destroyed. Rocket launches have become sporadic and ineffective—Hamas managed only two in September 2025, compared to thousands in 2023—due to depleted stockpiles and disrupted supply lines from Iran and other backers. Tunnel networks, a hallmark of Hamas's asymmetric warfare, have been largely neutralized through airstrikes and ground incursions.

- **Leadership and Command Structure**: High-profile eliminations, including Yahya Sinwar (Hamas's Gaza leader) in late 2024 and much of the political bureau in a September 2025 strike in Doha, Qatar, have left the group without unified command. Remaining leaders operate in hiding, relying on decentralized cells rather than coordinated offensives. This has shifted Hamas toward guerrilla tactics, such as ambushes on Israeli troops, but these lack the scale to threaten Israel's borders.

- **Resource Constraints**: Sanctions, border closures, and Israeli interdictions have severed external funding and arms flows. Iran's proxy network, including Hezbollah, has been weakened by parallel Israeli operations in Lebanon and Syria, leaving Hamas isolated. Disarmament remains a flashpoint in the ceasefire talks, with Israel demanding full demilitarization of Gaza as a condition for lasting peace.

#### The Ceasefire's Impact on Ongoing Capabilities
The first phase of the U.S.-brokered deal, ratified by Israel's cabinet on October 9 and effective as of October 10, includes:
- Israeli withdrawal from parts of northern and central Gaza.
- Hamas's release of approximately 20 living hostages and 25 bodies within 72 hours.
- A halt to hostilities, with reconstruction aid flowing into Gaza under international oversight.

This pause has frozen Hamas's remnants in place, preventing regrouping. Netanyahu has warned that any violation—such as rearmament—would trigger a return to war, emphasizing Gaza's demilitarization. Hamas, in turn, hails the deal as a "fruit of tremendous sacrifice" but insists on guarantees against renewed Israeli incursions. Analysts note that while the group can no longer mount large-scale attacks like October 7, low-level insurgency persists, and its survival has bolstered its narrative of resilience, aiding diplomatic gains like prisoner exchanges (over 250 Palestinians released so far).

#### Broader Context and Future Risks
Hamas's political arm continues to govern aspects of Gaza through alliances, but its military irrelevance has deepened Israel's strategic dominance. International observers, including the UN and SIPRI, highlight that the war has killed over 67,000 Palestinians and wounded 169,000, per Gaza's health ministry, underscoring the asymmetry. From an Israeli perspective, the conflict exposed intelligence failures but affirmed military superiority; from Palestinian viewpoints, it has isolated Israel globally while sustaining resistance ideology.

In summary, Hamas lacks the capacity for sustained warfare against Israel today—its forces are a shadow of their former selves, confined to survival mode under the ceasefire. Full eradication remains elusive, but without external resurgence, its threat level is minimal. The deal offers a pathway to de-escalation, though enforcement will test all parties.
