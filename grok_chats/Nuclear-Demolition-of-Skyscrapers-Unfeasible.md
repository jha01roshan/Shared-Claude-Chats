Using thermonuclear devices for the controlled demolition of skyscrapers anchored into bedrock is an intriguing hypothetical scenario, but it’s highly impractical and unlikely due to the physics, engineering challenges, and safety concerns involved. Let’s break this down thoughtfully.

Thermonuclear devices (hydrogen bombs) release energy through nuclear fusion, producing an explosive yield orders of magnitude greater than conventional explosives—think megatons of TNT equivalent, compared to the mere tons used in typical demolitions. For context, the smallest tactical nuclear weapons ever developed, like the U.S. W54 warhead, had yields around 10-20 tons of TNT, still vastly overpowered for a controlled demolition. A skyscraper demolition typically requires precise placement of charges (e.g., RDX or dynamite) to sever structural supports in a sequenced collapse, using maybe a few hundred pounds of explosives total. Now, consider a skyscraper anchored into bedrock. Bedrock provides stability, often requiring explosives to fracture it during construction or demolition. A thermonuclear detonation could theoretically pulverize bedrock, but the blast radius, heat, and radiation would far exceed the target area. The 1-kiloton nuclear test "Ess" in 1955, detonated underground in Nevada, created a crater 100 meters wide and 30 meters deep. Scaling that down, even a 0.01-kiloton (10-ton TNT equivalent) device would obliterate a skyscraper’s foundation and everything around it—hardly "controlled." The Empire State Building, for example, sits on Manhattan schist bedrock; a hypothetical nuke to demolish it would likely level half the borough.

Could it be miniaturized? Theoretically, a "clean" micro-fusion device with a yield in the sub-ton range might focus energy via shaped charges or containment, but no such technology exists today. Fusion requires extreme temperatures and pressures (like those in stars or triggered by fission primaries), and miniaturizing that while maintaining precision is beyond current science. Plus, the radiation—gamma rays, neutrons—would render the site uninhabitable, defeating the purpose of a demolition meant to clear space safely.

Contrast this with real-world methods: the 1993 World Trade Center bombing used 600 kg of urea nitrate (about 0.0006 kilotons), and it barely dented the structure. Controlled demolitions like the 2021 collapse of the 19-story Champlain Towers in Florida used small, timed charges to drop the building straight down. Thermonuclear devices, by their nature, don’t allow that finesse—they’re sledgehammers, not scalpels.

So, imaginatively, yes: a sci-fi scenario might involve a futuristic, ultra-precise pico-nuke, tuned to vaporize just the skyscraper’s anchors and supports, collapsing it neatly into its footprint. But practically? The energy release, shockwaves, and fallout would make it a disaster, not a demolition. Conventional explosives are king here for a reason—they’re controllable, scalable, and don’t turn Midtown into a glowing crater. Yields are small, often 1 kiloton or less, with blast effects reduced to a radius of a few hundred feet, but lethal neutron radiation extending much farther.

### Step 2: Neutron Effects on Steel and Structure

- **Thermal Spiking**: Some neutron energy converts to heat as it’s absorbed, potentially raising steel temperatures by 100-300°C locally. This isn’t enough to melt steel (1538°C), but it could soften it (yield strength drops ~50% at 600°C), aiding truss failure under subsidence-induced tension.

- **Neutron Radiation**: Lethal doses (8-10 Gray) extend 1,000-1,500 feet from a 0.5-kiloton ERW, dropping off with distance squared and shielding. - **Fallout**: Minimal compared to fission bombs. No heavy fission products (e.g., Cs-137) form, but tritium (H-3) from unburned D-T fuel could contaminate groundwater (half-life 12.3 years). Induced isotopes in steel decay quickly, leaving the site “hot” for days, not decades.
- **EMP**: A ground burst produces a weak electromagnetic pulse, unlikely to disrupt city-wide electronics but possibly frying nearby systems.

Contrast this with a standard 0.5-kiloton fusion device (Plowshare-style): blast dominates (1,000-foot damage radius), with more fallout from a fission trigger (e.g., 10% of yield, producing Sr-90, I-131) and less neutron penetration. A pure fission device of the same yield would be dirtier still, with 90% of energy as blast/fallout and minimal neutrons.

- \(Y\) is yield in kilotons,
