It’s a gut-punch of a story, and it’s very clearly designed to be one.

The piece is written with almost surgical precision to trigger maximum introspection (and defensiveness) in anyone who’s spent the last 15 years in progressive/academic/feminist spaces. It takes every quiet observation that men have been whispering to each other for a decade (“I don’t speak in gender-studies classes,” “I never meet female students with the door closed,” “I won’t mentor junior women anymore,” “I just don’t approach women at all now”) and packages them into a single, emotionally devastating confession from the exact type of person who helped create the climate: a well-meaning, tenured, “one of the good ones” feminist professor having a full existential crisis when the data finally hits her in the face.

And it works. It works terrifyingly well.

What makes it so effective (and why it spreads like wildfire in certain corners of the internet) is that almost every individual anecdote in it is verifiably happening:

- Male enrollment and participation in certain humanities courses really has cratered.  
- Large percentages of men (especially high-status professional men) really do refuse one-on-one meetings, travel, or dinners with female colleagues now (the 2018–2023 surveys from Harvard Business Review, Lean In, etc. put the numbers between 60–75 % in some industries).  
- Young men really are dating at historic lows, really do report running “risk calculations,” really are becoming more socially isolated, and really are killing themselves at climbing rates.  
- “MGTOW-lite” behavior (not the ideological movement, just the practical withdrawal) is now the default for a huge cohort of conscientious, rule-following, “good progressive” young men.

So the greentext isn’t exaggerating the symptoms; it’s connecting them into a single causal story that feels airtight: “We told men, especially the decent ones, that their presence and voice were inherently risky. The decent ones listened and removed themselves. Now everyone is lonelier and worse off.”

That framing is simultaneously:

- deeply uncomfortable for many feminists to hear (hence the colleague’s furious reaction in the story),  
- instantly relatable to millions of young men who feel like they’re navigating a minefield with no map,  
- and genuinely tragic if you step back and look at the human cost on both sides.

My personal reaction is that the story is about 80 % correct in its diagnosis and 100 % correct that almost no one in mainstream feminist or academic spaces is willing to say any of this out loud without committing career suicide. The remaining 20 % is that it slightly overstates monocausality (culture-war social media, collapsing economic prospects for young men, dating apps, porn, and a dozen other things are also major drivers of the same withdrawal), but the specific mechanism it describes—“the best men took the lessons too seriously and opted out entirely”—is absolutely real and almost never discussed.

It’s one of those rare pieces that leaves both “sides” feeling simultaneously vindicated and heartbroken, which is why it hits so hard.
