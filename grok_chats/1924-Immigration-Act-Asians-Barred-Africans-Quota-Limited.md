# 1924 Immigration Act: Asians Barred, Africans Quota-Limited

## Human

were asians and africas blocked entirely from obtaining visas under the INA Act of 1924

## Assistant

**No, the Immigration Act of 1924 (Johnson-Reed Act) did not block Asians and Africans entirely in the same way.**

### Asians
The Act **effectively banned nearly all immigration from Asia**. It did this by:
- Extending and reinforcing earlier restrictions (like the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, the 1917 Asiatic Barred Zone, and the Gentlemen's Agreement with Japan).
- Explicitly barring any "alien ineligible to citizenship" from admission as an immigrant. At the time, U.S. naturalization law (from 1790 onward, with later tweaks) limited citizenship to "free white persons" and people of "African nativity or descent." Courts had ruled Asians (including Japanese, Chinese, Indians, etc.) were racially ineligible for citizenship.<grok:render card_id="439955" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">10</argument></grok:render>

This created a near-total bar on new Asian immigrants (with narrow exceptions for diplomats, certain students, merchants, or family of existing residents in limited cases). The law is widely described as having "completely excluded immigrants from Asia" or "prohibited immigration from Asia."<grok:render card_id="bfeac4" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">0</argument></grok:render>

### Africans (or people from Africa)
The Act did **not** impose a total block on Africans or people from African countries. Instead:
- Most countries outside the Western Hemisphere (including those in Africa) received **national origins quotas**. These were initially set at 2% of the number of foreign-born residents of that nationality in the U.S. as of the 1890 census (later shifting to a national origins formula based on the 1920 census).
- African quotas were very small due to low prior immigration from Africa and how the formulas worked (e.g., excluding descendants of "slave immigrants" and others from the base population count, which boosted European quotas). One analysis notes that while ~9% of the U.S. population in 1920 was of African descent, less than 1% of the 1929 quotas went to African countries.<grok:render card_id="aa02db" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">1</argument></grok:render>
- Actual numbers were tiny: for example, data shows only about 1,200 quota visas annually for Africa in some periods under the system (far lower than for Europe).<grok:render card_id="b54bb1" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">27</argument></grok:render>

**Key distinction**: Africans were not racially barred from naturalization like Asians (people of African descent had been eligible for citizenship since the Naturalization Act of 1870). So they could qualify for quota visas if their country had an allotment and they met other requirements (visa from a U.S. consul, admissibility rules, etc.). Immigration from Africa was heavily restricted in practice but not "entirely blocked" as a legal category the way Asian immigration was.

### Additional Context
- The Act introduced the modern **visa requirement** (consular control system) for all immigrants and created the U.S. Border Patrol. It capped total Eastern Hemisphere immigration at ~165,000 initially (later ~150,000), with strong preferences for Northern/Western Europe.
- Western Hemisphere countries (Canada, Mexico, Latin America, etc.) were largely exempt from quotas as "non-quota immigrants."
- Some Black immigration from the Caribbean/West Indies faced extra hurdles because certain colonies were charged to European quotas, but this was quota manipulation rather than a total racial ban.
- The law's intent was to preserve the existing (mostly Northern European) racial and ethnic makeup of the U.S., influenced by eugenics ideas and nativism. It dramatically reduced overall immigration and remained the core framework until the 1965 Hart-Celler Act.<grok:render card_id="f5eea1" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">11</argument></grok:render>

In short: **Asians faced a near-total racial exclusion** under the "ineligible to citizenship" rule. **Africans faced severe quota limits** but not a complete bar—very few visas were available, but the law did not prohibit them outright on racial grounds. The Act treated the two groups differently due to existing citizenship laws.
