#### Historical Context of the 2008 Financial Crisis and Lehman Brothers  
The global financial crisis of 2007–2008 was marked by significant turmoil in financial markets, with Lehman Brothers, a major U.S. investment bank, filing for bankruptcy on September 15, 2008. This event is often cited as a pivotal moment in the crisis, leading to widespread economic instability. During this period, distressed assets like Lehman Brothers attracted interest from various international entities, including state-owned banks seeking investment opportunities.  

KDB, a state-owned South Korean bank, was reported to be in talks with Lehman Brothers for a possible investment or acquisition in August and September 2008. News articles from the time, such as those from [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2008/09/02/business/worldbusiness/02iht-kdb.15817700.html) and [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/2008/09/10/lehman-korean-bank-markets-equity-cx_tw_0910markets3.html), confirm that KDB considered forming a consortium with private banks to jointly buy Lehman, but the deal faced obstacles, including pricing gaps and domestic regulatory concerns.  

#### KDB’s Involvement and the Failed Deal  
According to multiple sources, KDB’s interest in Lehman Brothers was significant but ultimately unsuccessful. For instance, on August 22, 2008, Lehman’s shares rose 5% on reports of KDB’s potential interest, as noted in [Wikipedia - Bankruptcy of Lehman Brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bankruptcy_of_Lehman_Brothers). However, by September 9, 2008, KDB put talks on hold, leading to a 45% drop in Lehman’s stock price, as reported in [The Collapse of Lehman Brothers: A Case Study](https://www.investopedia.com/articles/economics/09/lehman-brothers-collapse.asp). The reasons cited included “differences over the terms of the deal” and “financial market conditions at home and overseas,” according to [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/2008/09/10/lehman-korean-bank-markets-equity-cx_tw_0910markets3.html).  

Domestic regulatory concerns in South Korea also played a role. For example, Jun Kwang Woo, chairman of South Korea’s Financial Services Commission, expressed in August 2008 that such an acquisition by a state-run bank might be “improper,” suggesting it should be led by the private sector, as detailed in [The Blows Continue At Lehman](https://www.forbes.com/2008/08/25/lehman-brothers-update-markets-equity-cx_cg_0825markets24.html). This indicates that internal governance and regulatory hurdles were significant barriers, rather than external pressures from foreign governments like China.
