## Assistant

In 1924, during debates and passage of the **Immigration Act of 1924** (also known as the Johnson-Reed Act or National Origins Act), **eugenics** was understood as the applied "science" of improving the genetic quality of the human population (or a specific national/racial stock) through selective breeding practices and policies. Proponents framed it as a biological and social tool to prevent "degeneration" of the American people by encouraging reproduction among those deemed "fit" while discouraging or blocking it among the "unfit."

### Core Definition at the Time
Eugenics, coined by Francis Galton in the late 19th century, was promoted in the U.S. as the **science of better breeding**. Charles Davenport, a leading figure and founder of the Eugenics Record Office, defined it along lines of "the improvement of the human race by better breeding." This included two main approaches:

- **Positive eugenics** — Encouraging higher birth rates and reproduction among individuals and groups considered genetically superior (often tied to traits like intelligence, health, industriousness, and moral character).
- **Negative eugenics** — Preventing reproduction among those viewed as genetically inferior or "socially inadequate" (e.g., through sterilization, marriage restrictions, or, in the immigration context, exclusion).

Eugenicists believed many human traits—intelligence, criminality, pauperism, feeblemindedness, insanity, alcoholism, and even "shiftlessness" or "insubordination"—were largely hereditary rather than environmental. They relied on family studies (like the Jukes or Kallikak families), early IQ testing (e.g., Henry Goddard's work at Ellis Island), institutionalization statistics, and crude anthropometric measurements to classify people.<grok:render card_id="b01a54" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">24</argument></grok:render>

### Application to the 1924 Immigration Act
In the specific context of the 1924 Act, eugenics was invoked to justify national-origin quotas based on the 1890 census (which heavily favored earlier waves of immigrants from Northern and Western Europe). The goal was to preserve the "racial" or "Nordic" character of the American population by limiting inflows from Southern and Eastern Europe (e.g., Italians, Jews, Slavs, Poles), whom eugenicists labeled as lower-quality stock likely to introduce "dysgenic" (genetically harmful) elements.

Key ideas included:
- **Racial hierarchy of Europeans**: Influential works like Madison Grant's *The Passing of the Great Race* (1916, widely circulated and cited in the 1920s) divided Europeans into "Nordic" (superior: tall, fair, blue-eyed, energetic, leadership-oriented), "Alpine," and "Mediterranean" (inferior) types. Grant and others warned of "race suicide" if superior Nordic stock was diluted by interbreeding or out-reproduced by "inferior" groups.<grok:render card_id="c43aa5" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">4</argument></grok:render>
- **Immigrants as a genetic threat**: Testimony before Congress, particularly from Harry H. Laughlin (superintendent of the Eugenics Record Office and designated "expert eugenics agent" to the House Immigration Committee), argued that recent immigrants showed higher rates of "feeblemindedness," crime, dependency, and other defects. Laughlin's data claimed Southern/Eastern European immigrants were "diluting the purity" of older Northern European American stock and burdening taxpayers.<grok:render card_id="d10ec3" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">0</argument></grok:render>
- **National preservation**: The Act was presented not merely as economic or cultural protectionism, but as a eugenic safeguard for the "American racial stock." President Calvin Coolidge echoed this in his signing statement, noting "racial considerations too grave to be brushed aside." Representative Albert Johnson (sponsor of the bill and tied to eugenics organizations) and Senator David Reed advanced similar views.

Eugenicists celebrated the law as a victory for "scientific" immigration policy. It drastically reduced overall immigration, set tiny quotas for Southern/Eastern Europe and Asia (effectively barring most Asians), and aimed to maintain the demographic balance favoring "desirable" (Nordic/Protestant Northern/Western European) stock.<grok:render card_id="2c43c4" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">20</argument></grok:render>

### Historical Context and Tools
This understanding of eugenics was mainstream in Progressive-era scientific and policy circles (supported by figures across political lines). It drew on emerging genetics, statistics, and psychology but rested heavily on assumptions of fixed racial differences and heritability of complex social behaviors—ideas later discredited as pseudoscience. It intersected with nativism, concerns over urbanization/industrialization, and post-WWI fears of radicalism and "overpopulation" by certain groups. The same movement also pushed for compulsory sterilization laws in many states.

In summary, in 1924 eugenics was not an abstract philosophy but a practical policy framework: use government tools (quotas, exclusion, later sterilization) to engineer a "better" population by favoring reproduction and entry of the "fit" (primarily Northern/Western European "Nordics") and restricting the "unfit." The Immigration Act was one of its most prominent legislative successes in the United States.
