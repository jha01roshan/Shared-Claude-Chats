## Assistant

**Miguel Díaz-Canel Bermúdez** (born April 20, 1960) is Cuba's current president and First Secretary of the Communist Party of Cuba (PCC), the country's top leadership positions. He is an electrical engineer by training and the first leader since the 1959 Cuban Revolution who is neither a Castro family member nor from the revolutionary generation.<grok:render card_id="e57e7d" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">11</argument></grok:render>

### Background and Rise
Born in Villa Clara province shortly after the revolution, Díaz-Canel is the son of a factory worker and a teacher. He graduated in electrical engineering, served in the military (including a period providing security for the Castros), taught at university, and rose through the ranks of the Young Communist League and the PCC. He held provincial party leadership roles, served as Minister of Higher Education, and became First Vice President under Raúl Castro. He was widely seen as a loyal, competent functionary rather than a charismatic revolutionary figure.<grok:render card_id="71b5ba" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">12</argument></grok:render>

### Election and Castro Family Support
Díaz-Canel was **not elected in a popular, competitive election**. Cuba operates as a one-party communist state under the PCC, with no legal opposition parties. In April 2018, the National Assembly (a body dominated by the PCC) selected him as the sole candidate to succeed Raúl Castro as President of the Council of State and Council of Ministers; he won with near-unanimous votes (e.g., 603-1). In 2019, following constitutional changes, he became President of the Republic. He was reelected in 2023 with over 97% of the Assembly's votes. In 2021, Raúl Castro stepped down as First Secretary of the PCC, and Díaz-Canel assumed that role as well.<grok:render card_id="84622f" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">46</argument></grok:render>

**Yes, he was strongly supported by the Castro family**, particularly Raúl Castro, who handpicked him as his successor to ensure continuity of the system. Raúl remained influential (as head of the PCC until 2021 and with family members still in key positions or the military). Díaz-Canel has been described as a "docile successor" tasked with maintaining Castroism amid economic challenges.<grok:render card_id="05c178" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">14</argument></grok:render>

### Governance and the Nature of the Regime
Under Díaz-Canel, Cuba remains a **one-party authoritarian state** with the PCC as the "superior guiding force" enshrined in the constitution. There are no free, fair, or competitive elections. His government has faced severe economic crises (exacerbated by the loss of Venezuelan oil subsidies, U.S. sanctions, the pandemic, and internal inefficiencies), leading to widespread shortages, blackouts, and mass protests (notably in July 2021).<grok:render card_id="123bfe" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">20</argument></grok:render>

**On repression**: Independent observers (Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, U.S. State Department, and Cuban dissident groups) consistently document ongoing systematic repression, including:
- Hundreds to over a thousand political prisoners.
- Arrests, trials without due process, and long sentences for protesters, activists, artists, and journalists (especially after 2021 protests).
- Censorship, restrictions on assembly and expression, internet controls, and harassment of independent voices.
- "Repudiation" acts and state security intimidation.<grok:render card_id="b648ae" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">0</argument></grok:render>

Díaz-Canel publicly called for revolutionaries to confront 2021 protesters ("The order of combat has been given"). While his administration introduced some social reforms (e.g., same-sex marriage via referendum, term limits for the presidency), these have not altered the fundamental one-party structure or ended political repression. Critics describe it as a continuation of Soviet-style control—centralized power, state dominance of the economy, suppression of dissent, and ideological conformity—though adapted to modern challenges like limited private enterprise and digital tools.<grok:render card_id="56c814" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">20</argument></grok:render>

Many Cubans face severe restrictions on leaving the country, economic hardship, and lack of basic freedoms, supporting descriptions of them as "prisoners in their own country" under an authoritarian system. Díaz-Canel has defended the regime, blamed external factors (especially the U.S. embargo), and rejected calls for systemic political change.<grok:render card_id="371f5b" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">36</argument></grok:render>

In summary, he represents managed continuity of the Castro-era system: loyal to the PCC, backed by Raúl, installed through internal party mechanisms, and presiding over a repressive single-party state that maintains tight political control despite economic failures and limited social openings.
