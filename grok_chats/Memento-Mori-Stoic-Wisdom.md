### Full A.R.A. Structure

**Executive Summary**  
Memento Mori (“remember that you must die”) names a contemplative discipline whose conceptual core is authentically Stoic even though the precise Latin phrase does not appear in the surviving works of Marcus Aurelius, Seneca, or Epictetus. The practice uses vivid awareness of mortality to reject procrastination in virtue, to diminish attachment to externals, and to focus ethical effort on the present—the only time that is ever ours. Primary text engagement with *Meditations* shows this functions as a psychological and ethical tool, not a source of paralysis. It directly supports the Stoic commitments to the dichotomy of control, the primacy of virtue, and acceptance of natural processes (including death).

**Primary Text Engagement (Meditations)**

Direct passage (Book 2.11):  
“You could leave life right now. Let that determine what you do and say and think.”

This is self-addressed and operational. By framing the present moment as potentially final, Marcus installs an immediate ethical filter that privileges actions consistent with reason and character over those driven by fear, desire, or reputation. [Primary basis: direct quotation from *Meditations*.]

Direct passage (Book 4.17):  
“Death overshadows you. While you’re alive and able—be good.”

The reminder of mortality is explicitly yoked to the imperative for right action *while capacity exists*. The clause “while you’re alive and able” is crucial: it rejects deferral and centers agency in the present. [Primary basis: direct quotation from *Meditations*.]

Additional primary texture appears in Marcus’ repeated reflections on the swift erasure of even the most powerful figures (e.g., earlier emperors). These passages illustrate how external honors and ambitions are rendered meaningless by time, redirecting attention inward to one’s own moral condition. [Primary basis: thematic pattern across multiple books of *Meditations*.]

**Interpretive Claim Flag**  
The specific phrase “Memento Mori” and its later visual culture (skulls, hourglasses, vanitas still lifes) developed significantly after the classical Stoic period, especially in medieval and Baroque Christian contexts. The Stoic contribution is the internalized, action-oriented version visible in the primary texts above. No claim is made that Marcus Aurelius used the Latin slogan itself.

**Practice in Formal Terms**  
A disciplined Stoic approach includes:

- Pre-action or pre-decision reflection: Invoke the awareness that time is limited to test whether the contemplated move aligns with virtue.
- Journaling modeled on Marcus’ own method: Regular written contemplation of impermanence to maintain perspective and reduce reactive distortion.
- Evening review: Assess the day through the lens “Did I act as one who could leave life now?”
- Integration with other Stoic exercises (e.g., *premeditatio malorum*) to strengthen acceptance of what is not up to us, including death itself.

The logical outcome, per the primary logic, is reduced attachment to indifferents, greater courage in duty, and heightened presence for the opportunity to live well while it remains available.
