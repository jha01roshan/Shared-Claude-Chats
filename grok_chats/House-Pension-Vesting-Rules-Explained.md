## Human

Is it true that representatives in the House will only get a life time pension of they have hold a seat for 5 years exactly?

---

## Assistant

No, the claim is not true. Members of the U.S. House of Representatives (and Congress more broadly) become vested in their federal pension under the Federal Employees Retirement System (FERS, which applies to all current members) after completing **at least** five years of creditable service, not **exactly** five years. This vesting means they are legally entitled to a deferred pension benefit upon reaching the applicable retirement age, regardless of whether they serve longer.

### Key Details on Eligibility and Benefits
- **Vesting Requirement**: Five years of service establishes the right to a future pension. Service less than five years (e.g., a single two-year term) results in no pension entitlement.
- **Retirement Age and Payout Start**:
  - At age 62 with 5+ years of service: Eligible for an immediate unreduced annuity.
  - At age 50 with 20+ years: Eligible for an immediate annuity (with potential reductions if taken early).
  - With 25+ years: Eligible for an immediate annuity at any age.
- **Pension Calculation**: The annual amount is based on years of service × 1% (or 1.7% if retiring at 62+ with 20+ years) × the average of the highest three years of salary (typically around $174,000 for rank-and-file members). It can reach up to 80% of final salary but is not a "lifetime pension" at full pay—it's a defined benefit annuity paid monthly for life, supplemented by Social Security and optional Thrift Savings Plan contributions.
- **Historical Note**: Members elected before 1984 could opt into the older Civil Service Retirement System (CSRS), which has similar five-year vesting but different age/service rules (e.g., age 60 with 10 years). However, all members today are under FERS.

## Human

So how much will MTG get when she retires in January?

---

## Assistant

Marjorie Taylor Greene (MTG) announced her resignation from the U.S. House of Representatives effective January 5, 2026, after serving since January 3, 2021. This gives her exactly five years and three days of creditable service—barely meeting the five-year vesting threshold under the Federal Employees Retirement System (FERS) for a deferred pension. She is currently 51 years old (born May 27, 1974) and not eligible for immediate retirement benefits, as she lacks the required age (62 for a deferred annuity with 5+ years) or additional service years for earlier options.

### Pension Amount and Start Date
- **Annual Pension**: Approximately **$8,700** per year (or about $725 monthly), starting at age 62 in 2036. This is a lifetime annuity, adjusted annually for inflation via cost-of-living adjustments (COLAs).
- **How It's Calculated**:
  - Formula: 1% × years of service × high-3 average salary.
  - Years of service: 5.
  - High-3 average salary: $174,000 (the 2021–2025 salary for rank-and-file House members, unchanged since 2009).
  - Math: 1% × 5 × $174,000 = 0.05 × $174,000 = **$8,700**.
- **Lifetime Value**: Estimates suggest this could total over $265,000 in present-value terms, assuming average life expectancy and COLAs, though actual payouts depend on inflation and longevity.
- **Options**: She could instead take a lump-sum refund of her FERS contributions (employee portion only, around 0.8% of salary per year, or roughly $7,000 total, plus interest) and forfeit the annuity. No immediate payout occurs in January 2026.

### Other Retirement Benefits
FERS includes three components, all of which MTG qualifies for:
- **Deferred Annuity**: As detailed above.
- **Social Security**: Fully covered during her service; her benefit will factor in her overall career earnings (not just congressional salary).
- **Thrift Savings Plan (TSP)**: A 401(k)-style account with up to 5% employer match. Her balance (from contributions since 2021) can be withdrawn, rolled over, or left invested post-retirement, but the exact amount is private and not publicly disclosed.

This modest pension reflects her short tenure; longer-serving members (e.g., 20+ years) can receive up to 80% of final salary. Critics, including Laura Loomer and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, have called the timing "greedy," but it's standard under congressional rules. For official FERS details, see the Office of Personnel Management.
