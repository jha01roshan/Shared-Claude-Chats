Is ‘journalism’ getting more trustworthy as a result the citizen journalism boom?

## Assistant

No, **journalism** as a whole is not getting more trustworthy as a result of the **citizen journalism** boom. In fact, broad public trust in media has continued to decline or stagnate at historic lows in recent years, even as citizen-generated content has proliferated via social media, smartphones, and platforms like X, YouTube, and TikTok.<grok:render card_id="c5e660" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">4</argument></grok:render>

### Trust Trends in Media
Recent polling shows persistent erosion:
- **Gallup (2025)**: Only 28% of Americans express a "great deal" or "fair amount" of trust in mass media (newspapers, TV, radio) to report news "fully, accurately, and fairly"—a new low, down from 31% the prior year and far below historical highs like 68% in the 1970s. Republicans are at just 8%, Democrats at 51%, with independents low as well.<grok:render card_id="293d85" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">25</argument></grok:render>
- **Pew Research (2025)**: Trust in national news organizations fell to 56% (down 11 points in months and 20 since 2016); local news holds at ~70% but has also declined. Trust in social media sources remains lower and more volatile.<grok:render card_id="9872e8" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">0</argument></grok:render>
- Broader patterns (Edelman, Reuters Institute, YouGov 2024–2025): Media trust is low or declining in many countries, with polarization playing a major role—people trust outlets aligning with their views more than "journalism" in general. Younger people and heavy social media users often show even lower confidence in legacy outlets.<grok:render card_id="dd291d" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">45</argument></grok:render>

This decline predates the full citizen journalism explosion (roots in the 1970s–1990s with polarization, economic pressures on newsrooms, and cable/news fragmentation), but the boom has coincided with and arguably accelerated scrutiny rather than reversing it.

### What Citizen Journalism Brings
**Citizen journalism**—raw footage, eyewitness accounts, on-the-ground reporting by non-professionals—has clear upsides:
- **Speed and access**: It fills gaps where professionals can't or won't go (e.g., disaster zones, censored regions, protests, or hyper-local stories). Iconic images and videos from events like the Arab Spring, police incidents, or conflicts often come from citizens first.
- **Accountability pressure**: It acts as a distributed watchdog. Viral citizen clips or threads have exposed errors, biases, or underreported angles in mainstream coverage, forcing corrections or follow-ups. Some studies note it can enhance credibility when it corroborates or challenges official narratives, especially in low-trust environments.
- **Diversity**: More voices, perspectives, and unfiltered raw material broaden the information ecosystem beyond editorial gatekeepers.

In theory, this competition and crowdsourced verification could incentivize better practices: mainstream outlets now routinely incorporate citizen content, and public scrutiny (via comments, shares, fact-checks) raises the cost of obvious mistakes.

### The Countervailing Realities
Empirical outcomes point to net challenges for overall trustworthiness:
- **Misinformation and quality issues**: Without professional training, editorial standards, or accountability mechanisms (e.g., corrections policies, legal/ethical constraints), citizen content often spreads faster than it can be verified. Studies highlight problems with bias, unverified claims, activism disguised as reporting, and outright errors or disinformation. One analysis notes citizen journalism's "speedy, unpretentious" appeal but flags "major trustworthiness issues caused by misinformation and bias," eroding ecosystem-wide trust.<grok:render card_id="7d68a2" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">1</argument></grok:render> Surveys show many view it as less credible than professional work, though younger/digital-native groups engage it more.
- **Fragmentation and polarization**: The boom amplifies echo chambers. People gravitate to citizen sources (or influencers) that confirm priors, while legacy media faces accusations of gatekeeping or bias. Result: Lower aggregate trust, not higher. Social media as a news vector correlates with news avoidance, burnout, and skepticism of *all* sources.
- **No clear trust rebound**: Despite decades of citizen input (blogs in the 2000s, Twitter/X videos, TikTok reports), trust metrics haven't improved meaningfully. Some research suggests the rise of user-generated content disrupted gatekeeping in ways that increased perceived unreliability overall, even as it democratized access. Mainstream outlets sometimes amplify unvetted citizen material for speed/engagement, compounding problems.
- **Mixed perceptions**: Citizen journalism shines for raw authenticity and holding power accountable but struggles with objectivity, fact-checking depth, and consistency. Professional journalism retains advantages in resources, verification infrastructure, and (ideally) norms of balance—yet faces its own critiques of institutional capture, economic incentives (clicks over accuracy), and ideological slant.

### Bottom Line: More Scrutiny, Not More Trust
The citizen boom has made the information environment **more competitive and transparent** in some ways—raw evidence circulates widely, errors get called out faster, and monopolies on narrative are harder to maintain. This can pressure "journalism" (professional or otherwise) toward improvement over time, much like open-source code benefits from many eyes.

However, it has not translated into higher trustworthiness for journalism broadly. Instead, it has contributed to a noisier, more contested space where discerning signal from noise requires more individual effort (media literacy, cross-verification, source evaluation). Trust remains low because incentives—speed, engagement, tribal alignment, algorithmic amplification—often reward sensationalism or partial truths over rigorous accuracy, whether from citizens or professionals.

True gains in trustworthiness would require better verification norms across the board (e.g., timestamped raw footage, transparent sourcing, willingness to correct), reduced polarization in consumption, and cultural emphasis on skepticism without cynicism. Citizen journalism helps expose flaws but doesn't automatically fix the underlying human and structural issues driving distrust. The public is rightly more skeptical; whether that leads to better journalism depends on how all participants respond.

Are their any surveys like Gallup that help substantiate the mistrust in 'Citizen Journalism new" vs Corporate news?

## Assistant

Yes, several reputable surveys from organizations like **Pew Research Center**, **Reuters Institute Digital News Report**, **Edelman Trust Barometer**, **YouGov**, and **Emerson College Polling** provide data on public perceptions of trust in different news sources. However, **direct head-to-head comparisons** of "citizen journalism" (or user-generated content, social media news, eyewitness reports by non-professionals) versus "corporate/mainstream/traditional journalism" are relatively rare in large-scale, ongoing polls like Gallup's annual media trust tracker.

Most surveys measure trust in **professional news organizations** (national/local outlets, TV, newspapers) versus **social media platforms** or **online sources**, where much citizen journalism circulates. Citizen journalism itself is often not named explicitly as a category, but it overlaps heavily with user-generated content, videos/posts from ordinary people, and non-professional sharing on platforms like X, TikTok, YouTube, or Facebook.

### Key Patterns from Major Surveys
Trust in **professional/corporate news** (mainstream outlets) is generally higher than in social media or unfiltered user-generated sources, though both have faced declines or skepticism. People often view citizen content as faster/raw/more authentic for breaking events but less reliable overall due to verification gaps, bias, or misinformation risks.

- **Pew Research Center** (ongoing U.S. surveys, including 2025 data):  
  Trust in information from **national news organizations** stands at 56% ("a lot" or "some" trust) as of late 2025—down sharply in recent months and 20 points since 2016. **Local news organizations** fare better at ~70%. Trust in information from **social media sites** is consistently and substantially lower (often in the 30-40% range, with Republicans and Democrats showing similar low levels in some snapshots). Pew notes a "trust gap" where people are more skeptical of news encountered via digital platforms versus news from established outlets.<grok:render card_id="e71379" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">33</argument></grok:render>

- **Reuters Institute Digital News Report** (annual, multi-country, 2025 edition):  
  Overall trust in "the news" hovers around 40% globally and has been stable but low. When verifying suspicious information online, people most often turn to **trusted news brands** (38%) or official sources—far ahead of **social media/video networks** (lower single digits in many cases) or comments from others. Social platforms like Facebook, TikTok, and X are frequently cited as major sources of **mis/disinformation threats** (e.g., 49% for Facebook globally). The report highlights that audiences see news on platforms as more questionable than news media in general, partly due to the mix of professional and amateur/unverified content.<grok:render card_id="cae070" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">32</argument></grok:render>

- **Edelman Trust Barometer** (global, 2025):  
  Trust in **traditional media** is higher than in **social media** (which scores notably lower, often in the 40s or below). "Owned media" (corporate channels) sometimes performs differently, but overall, social sources lag. A recurring theme is concern that it's getting harder to distinguish respected media from deceptive individual posts—directly relevant to citizen journalism's unfiltered nature. Trust in media as an institution remains challenged across many countries.<grok:render card_id="272c94" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">41</argument></grok:render>

- **YouGov** (U.S. trust in media polls, 2023–2025):  
  Specific news outlets (mostly professional/corporate) show varying net trust scores, often polarized by party. **Social media platforms** as news sources score poorly overall: more Americans rate news on Facebook, TikTok, Instagram, etc., as untrustworthy than trustworthy. YouTube and LinkedIn sometimes edge slightly higher, but platforms generally underperform traditional outlets. Younger adults (<45) show somewhat higher tolerance for social media news, but skepticism remains widespread.<grok:render card_id="c8ccb7" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">51</argument></grok:render>

- **Emerson College Polling** (2025 U.S. national media poll):  
  **Local news** tops trust at 72% ("great deal/fair amount"), followed by **national news organizations** at 62%. **Social media/YouTube** lags at 46%—the lowest among major categories—yet it serves as the **main news source** for 37% of Americans (and 64% of those under 30). This highlights a clear "trust-use gap": heavy reliance on citizen-influenced platforms despite lower confidence.<grok:render card_id="a3bfea" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">72</argument></grok:render>

- **Other academic/niche surveys**:  
  A 2025 quantitative survey of 486 self-reported citizen journalism participants found that while citizen reporting is seen as speedy and influential, it faces "major trustworthiness issues caused by misinformation and bias." Respondents and observers often rate professional practices higher for consistency and verification. Older studies (e.g., on perceptions during events) sometimes note raw citizen footage gaining quick credibility for authenticity, but edited/professional framing retaining an edge for depth and balance.

### Why the Data Is Indirect but Substantiating
Gallup-style polls rarely isolate "citizen journalism" as a standalone category (it lacks the institutional branding of "mass media"). Instead, proxies like **social media news consumption** or **user-generated content** consistently show lower trust metrics than **professional journalism outlets**. This aligns with broader findings that the public differentiates: mainstream/corporate sources benefit from perceived editorial standards, resources, and accountability (even if eroded by bias critiques), while citizen journalism shines in raw access but struggles with verification, consistency, and perceived partisanship/activism.

Polarization plays a big role—trust is often higher for sources aligning with one's views, whether legacy or independent. Misinformation concerns amplify skepticism toward unvetted citizen material, even as people value its speed in crises.

In short, these surveys substantiate higher relative mistrust in citizen journalism/new (via social/user-generated channels) compared to corporate/professional news, though neither enjoys robust public confidence overall. Trust hasn't rebounded with the citizen boom; the ecosystem feels noisier, pushing more individual verification burden onto audiences. For the most current nuances, check the latest Pew, Reuters Institute, or YouGov releases, as figures shift yearly and by demographics.
