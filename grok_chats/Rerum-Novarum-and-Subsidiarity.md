## Assistant

**Pope Leo XIII (born Gioacchino Vincenzo Raffaele Luigi Pecci, 1810–1903)** served as pope from February 1878 until his death in July 1903—one of the longest pontificates in history (25 years, the third-longest after St. Peter and Pius IX). An intellectual and diplomat from Italian minor nobility, he succeeded the more defensive Pius IX and sought to engage the modern world constructively after the loss of the Papal States. He earned the titles “Social Pope” and “Pope of the Workers” for pioneering modern Catholic social teaching, particularly through *Rerum Novarum*. He was also called the “Rosary Pope” for issuing a record 11 encyclicals on Marian devotion and the rosary (plus approving new scapulars). Other distinctions include reviving Thomistic philosophy (*Aeterni Patris*, 1879), opening the Vatican Secret Archives to scholars, composing the Prayer to St. Michael the Archangel, producing 86 encyclicals total (modernizing the encyclical as a teaching tool), and becoming the first pope to have his voice recorded and to appear on film. He emphasized the Church’s role in addressing contemporary social, political, and economic issues while upholding natural law and human dignity.<grok:render card_id="409014" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">10</argument></grok:render><grok:render card_id="fd1a0b" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">14</argument></grok:render>

**The purpose of *Rerum Novarum* (“Of New Things,” issued May 15, 1891)** was to provide the Church’s authoritative response to the “social question”—the widespread misery, exploitation, and class conflict caused by the Industrial Revolution. It addressed the condition of the working classes directly, rejecting both atheistic socialism (which it said destroys private property, the family, and natural rights) and unchecked laissez-faire capitalism (which treats workers as mere instruments for profit). Instead, it defended workers’ rights to a just (living) wage, safe conditions, reasonable hours, the formation of trade unions or associations, and private property as a natural right. It stressed the mutual duties of capital and labor, the dignity of work, the role of Christian charity and morality in resolving conflicts, and limited but necessary state intervention for the common good. The Church positioned itself as the moral guide and advocate for justice, using religion to foster harmony rather than revolution.<grok:render card_id="3851f8" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">0</argument></grok:render>

Leo XIII felt it was historically urgent to publish it in 1891 because the Industrial Revolution had dismantled medieval guilds and protections, leaving workers “surrendered, isolated and helpless” to greedy employers and cutthroat competition amid rapid urbanization, mechanization, and wealth concentration. This fueled rising socialism, communism, and labor unrest across Europe and beyond, threatening social order and the family. Earlier papal statements had touched on related issues, but Leo saw the need for a detailed, systematic treatment to refute false ideologies, prevent misapprehension, and offer a positive Catholic alternative rooted in natural law, justice, and the Gospel—before radical agitators could exploit the crisis further. As he noted in the opening, “some opportune remedy must be found quickly for the misery and wretchedness pressing so unjustly on the majority of the working class.”<grok:render card_id="498e47" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">45</argument></grok:render>

**Rerum Novarum articulates the principle of subsidiarity** (though the term itself was formalized later by Pius XI in *Quadragesimo Anno*, 1931) by insisting that higher authorities (especially the state) must not absorb or override what individuals, families, local groups, private associations, or charities can and should do themselves. The family is presented as a true society “antecedent… to the gathering of men into a community,” with its own natural rights and authority that predate and are independent of the state. The state exists to support, not supplant, these lower orders.<grok:render card_id="08c00f" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">45</argument></grok:render>

Key verbatim passages include:

- Paragraph 35: “We have said that the State must not absorb the individual or the family; both should be allowed free and untrammelled action so far as is consistent with the common good and the interest of others.”<grok:render card_id="5867e3" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">47</argument></grok:render>

- Paragraph 36: “Whenever the general interest or any particular class suffers, or is threatened with harm, which can in no other way be met or prevented, the public authority must step in to deal with it… the law must not undertake more, nor proceed further, than is required for the remedy of the evil or the removal of the mischief.”<grok:render card_id="5c5cc8" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">47</argument></grok:render>

- Paragraph 14: “The contention… that the civil government should at its option intrude into and exercise intimate control over the family and the household is a great and pernicious error… Paternal authority can be neither abolished nor absorbed by the State… [except in] extreme necessity.” It also defends private associations (like workingmen’s unions) as natural rights that the state must protect, not prohibit or absorb.<grok:render card_id="e42381" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">45</argument></grok:render>

This framework—lower levels first, higher levels only as a “subsidy” (help) when truly needed—became foundational to Catholic social teaching.

**In the Catholic Church’s own operations**, subsidiarity is lived out as a guiding principle of governance, ministry, and social action: matters are handled at the most local competent level (individual conscience, family as “domestic church,” parish, diocese, or religious order) unless higher authority is required for the common good. Bishops exercise real authority in their dioceses without Vatican micromanagement; pastors lead parishes with autonomy; lay Catholics, Catholic charities (e.g., Caritas), schools, hospitals, and apostolates operate with freedom suited to local needs, supported but not supplanted by higher structures. Religious orders and lay movements retain their charisms and internal governance. Canon law and the Church’s social doctrine reinforce this: the Vatican or universal Church sets doctrine and coordinates on global issues, but encourages initiative, creativity, and responsibility at every level. This mirrors *Rerum Novarum*’s vision—empowering smaller communities (families, local groups, charities) to meet needs first, with the larger body intervening only to assist or remedy failures—fostering human dignity, freedom, and effective service rather than centralized bureaucracy.<grok:render card_id="21d684" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">21</argument></grok:render>

In short, *Rerum Novarum* marked a pivotal moment when the Church, through Leo XIII’s vision, offered a balanced, humane alternative to the extremes of 19th-century economics and politics—one that continues to shape both societal teaching and the Church’s internal life today.
